## 1. The Professional Naming Taxonomy

Professional naming agencies (Igor, Lexicon, Heirloom/How Brands Are Built) reject flat lists of "types of names." Instead, they use a **two-axis chart** that captures virtually every brand name type ([How Brands Are Built](https://howbrandsarebuilt.com/types-of-brand-names/)):

### The Two Axes

**Vertical axis -- Approach (what the name communicates):**
- **Descriptive** -- directly tells you what the company does (PayPal, General Electric)
- **Suggestive/Evocative** -- hints at what the company does through metaphor or association (Slack, Stripe, Amazon)
- **Abstract** -- has no inherent connection to the product (Apple, Kodak, Xerox)

**Horizontal axis -- Construct (how the name is built):**
- **Real word** -- an existing dictionary word used as-is (Apple, Slack, Square)
- **Compound** -- two words combined (Airbnb, Snapchat, Facebook)
- **Coined** -- an invented word with no prior meaning (Google, Xerox, Spotify)

### Where Specific Name Types Fall

| Strategy | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Founders/Heritage | Named after a person | Bloomberg, Goldman Sachs, Hewlett-Packard |
| Geographical | Named after a place | Patagonia, Amazon, Cisco |
| Metaphorical | Real word used as metaphor (a subset of suggestive names) | Apple, Stripe, Nike, Amazon |
| Acronym | Abbreviation of descriptive name | IBM, AWS, KFC |
| Portmanteau | Blending two word parts | Pinterest (pin + interest), Instagram (instant + telegram) |
| Truncation | Shortened real word | Cisco (San Francisco), Fedex (Federal Express) |
| Misspelling | Intentional respelling | Lyft, Fiverr, Tumblr |
| Empty vessel | Coined word with zero prior meaning | Kodak, Rolex, Haagen-Dazs |

Source: Rob Meyerson, author of *Brand Naming* and co-author of the 6th edition of *Designing Brand Identity* ([How Brands Are Built](https://howbrandsarebuilt.com/types-of-brand-names/))

## 2. The Science of Sound Symbolism

This is the most research-backed area of naming science. Sound symbolism is the finding that individual speech sounds carry inherent, cross-cultural meaning -- independent of the words they form.

### The Bouba/Kiki Effect

In Wolfgang Kohler's landmark experiment, 95-98% of people across languages and cultures assigned the word "bouba" to a rounded shape and "kiki" to a jagged shape. This demonstrates that humans instinctively map sounds to physical properties ([SpellBrand](https://spellbrand.com/blog/psychology-behind-brand-names)).

### The Phonetic Toolkit

Research from Lexicon Branding founder David Placek, Stanford, and multiple published studies identifies specific sound-meaning associations ([Branding Mag](https://www.brandingmag.com/david-placek/the-science-of-sound-symbolism-and-the-importance-of-your-brand-name/), [JSTOR Daily](https://daily.jstor.org/whats-brand-name-sounds-persuasion/), [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0148296322005458)):

**Vowels:**

| Sound | Perception | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Front vowels (i, ee, e) | Small, light, fast, precise, sharp, energetic | Visa, Kindle, Wii, Lyft |
| Back vowels (o, oo, ah) | Large, heavy, slow, warm, powerful, round | Volvo, Google, Roku, Zoom |
| Open vowels (ah, oh) | Expansive, luxurious, open | Sonos, Chanel, Dior |

**Consonants:**

| Sound | Perception | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Plosives (b, p, d, t, g, k) | Bold, strong, decisive, energetic | BlackBerry, Pentium, TikTok |
| Fricatives (f, v, sh, zh, s, z) | Smooth, flowing, elegant, fast | Visa, Swiffer, Shazam |
| Nasals (m, n) | Warm, soft, comforting | Marriott, Muji, Amazon |
| Liquids (l, r) | Flowing, liquid, smooth | Loreal, Revlon, Lush |
| Voiceless stops (p, t, k) | Hard, sharp, crisp, efficient | Stripe, Slack, Kraft |
| Voiced stops (b, d, g) | Softer, warmer than voiceless | Google, Bose, Dell |

**Industry-specific sound patterns** identified by Lexicon Branding ([Branding Mag](https://www.brandingmag.com/david-placek/the-science-of-sound-symbolism-and-the-importance-of-your-brand-name/)):
- **Automotive** (energy, speed, action): j, p, sh, k, v -- Porsche, Jaguar, Corvette
- **Hotels** (comfort, warmth): m, h, w, l, r, n -- Marriott, Hilton, Wyndham, Hyatt
- **Cosmetics** (luxury, softness): same as hotels -- Loreal, Maybelline, Aramis, Revlon
- **Tech** (precision, efficiency): sharp consonants, front vowels -- Pixel, Twitch, Slack, Stripe

### Consonant-Vowel Pattern (CV Structure)

Lexicon Branding's David Placek identifies that the most naturally pronounceable names use the **consonant-vowel-consonant-vowel pattern** that mirrors how babies first learn to speak (mama, dada, baba). Names built on this scaffolding feel inherently natural and easy across languages. Example: Dasani uses the CV structure and places "san" (Latin root for health) at its center ([Branding Mag](https://www.brandingmag.com/david-placek/the-science-of-sound-symbolism-and-the-importance-of-your-brand-name/)).

### Visual Symbolism in Spelling

Lexicon's research also found:
- **Doubled consonants** (e.g., Google vs. Gugle) make a product seem to have more options and features
- **Palindromes** (Sonos, Kayak) are visually distinctive and memorable
- The visual shape of letters matters -- round letters (o, g, b) feel softer than angular ones (k, x, z)

Source: David Placek, founder of Lexicon Branding (the firm behind Pentium, BlackBerry, Sonos, Swiffer, Dasani, Impossible Foods) ([Branding Mag](https://www.brandingmag.com/david-placek/the-science-of-sound-symbolism-and-the-importance-of-your-brand-name/))

## 3. Ideal Length: What the Data Says

### Character Count

Research from Harvard Business School, cognitive psychology studies, and analysis of thousands of startups converges on a consistent finding ([Frozen Lemons](https://www.frozenlemons.com/blog/how-long-should-business-name-be)):

| Metric | Optimal Range | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Characters | **4-8 letters** | Harvard Business School, Frozen Lemons analysis |
| Syllables | **1-2 syllables ideal, 3 maximum** | ResearchGate phonetic symbolism study, Forbes |
| Words | **1 word preferred, 2 max** | BrandBucket analysis |

### Why Shorter Works: The Cognitive Science

- **Miller's Rule**: Human working memory holds 7 plus or minus 2 items. Names within 4-8 characters get processed as a single "chunk" ([Frozen Lemons](https://www.frozenlemons.com/blog/how-long-should-business-name-be))
- **Processing fluency**: A 2006 study by psychologist Adam Alter found companies with easier-to-pronounce stock ticker symbols outperformed those with difficult symbols in the days after IPO ([SpellBrand](https://spellbrand.com/blog/psychology-behind-brand-names))
- **Name fluency and market value**: Research found each improvement in name fluency correlated with a **2.53% increase in market-to-book ratio**, translating to approximately **$3.75 million in added market value** for median-sized firms ([Frozen Lemons](https://www.frozenlemons.com/blog/how-long-should-business-name-be))
- **The Law of Three Syllables**: Names exceeding 3 syllables invariably get shortened in practice -- International House of Pancakes becomes IHOP, Federal Express becomes FedEx, Kentucky Fried Chicken becomes KFC ([Frozen Lemons](https://www.frozenlemons.com/blog/how-long-should-business-name-be))

### The Evidence in Practice

Top tech companies cluster in the 4-6 letter range:
- Apple (5), Google (6), Meta (4), Tesla (5), Uber (4), Zoom (4), Stripe (6), Slack (5), Plaid (5), Square (6), Mint (4), Etsy (4)

Research finding: **2-3 syllable names were more memorable (M=4.12) than longer names (M=3.45, p<0.01)** -- ResearchGate, "Phonetic Symbolism and Brand Name Preference" ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/23547390_Phonetic_Symbolism_and_Brand_Name_Preference))

### Exception: Luxury Names Can Be Longer

A study published in *Psychology & Marketing* found that **as brand name length increases from mono-syllabic to bi-syllabic or tri-syllabic, the perception of luxuriousness increases** ([Wiley - Psychology & Marketing](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/mar.21247)). This is because longer names create an "illusion of rarity." Examples: Balenciaga, Lamborghini, Brunello Cucinelli.

## 4. Premium vs. Cheap: What Makes a Name Sound Expensive

### The Late-Acquired Phoneme Theory

Research from Nanyang Technological University (NTU Singapore) by Abhishek Pathak and Gemma Calvert provides one of the most rigorous frameworks for what makes a name sound luxurious vs. basic ([NTU Singapore](https://www.ntu.edu.sg/business/news-events/news/story-detail/luxury-brands-and-linguistics-what-does-luxury-sound-like)):

**Core finding**: Sounds that children learn late (after age 5) -- such as "sh," "ch," "zh," "th" -- signal luxury. Sounds learned early (before age 2) -- such as "m," "b," "d," "p" -- signal basic/everyday products.

**Why it works**: Early-acquired sounds feel familiar, simple, concrete. Late-acquired sounds feel distant, rare, abstract -- the same qualities we associate with luxury.

**Experimental proof**: By simply adding a late-acquired phoneme (like "sh") to a hypothetical brand name, researchers could flip consumer perception from basic to luxury. Respondents categorized significantly more luxury product categories for names with late-acquired phonemes.

### Affricates: The Luxury Sound

**Affricates** (consonant sounds causing excessive friction, like "ch" in Gucci and "z" in Haagen-Dazs) are disproportionately common in luxury brand names despite being considered "unpleasant" in everyday names. The unconventional quality is precisely what conveys exclusivity and distinction ([NTU Singapore](https://www.ntu.edu.sg/business/news-events/news/story-detail/luxury-brands-and-linguistics-what-does-luxury-sound-like)).

### Premium Name Signals

| Signals "Premium" | Signals "Cheap" |
|---|---|
| Flowing sounds, open vowels (Chanel, Dior) | Plosives, abrupt sounds |
| Late-acquired phonemes (sh, ch, zh, th) | Early-acquired phonemes (m, b, p, d) |
| 2-3 syllables for luxury ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/mar.21247)) | Monosyllabic can feel basic |
| Absence of adjectives like "best," "pro," "affordable" in the name ([eBusiness Blog](https://www.ebusinessblog.co.uk/why-do-some-stores-sound-expensive/)) | Adjectives like "best," "pro," "value" in the name |
| Latin/Greek/French etymological roots | Plain English descriptors |
| Unconventional spelling (Haagen-Dazs) | Completely phonetic spelling |
| No exclamation marks or ALL CAPS | Excessive punctuation |

## 5. The Major Evaluation Frameworks

### Framework 1: Igor Naming Agency -- The Nine Criteria

Igor (the agency behind Aria, Gogo, TruTV) evaluates every name candidate on nine dimensions, scored 0-10 each (90 max) ([Igor International](https://www.igorinternational.com/process/name-evaluation-brand-evaluate-names.php)):

1. **Appearance** -- How the name looks visually (logo, billboard, ad)
2. **Distinctive** -- How differentiated from competitors
3. **Depth** -- Layers of meaning and association ("names with great depth never reveal all they have to offer at once, but keep surprising you with new ideas")
4. **Energy** -- Vitality, buzz, ability to carry an ad campaign
5. **Humanity** -- Warmth, "humanness" (test: could this be a nickname for your child?)
6. **Positioning** -- Relevance to the brand's positioning strategy
7. **Sound** -- How it sounds when spoken AND how easily it is spoken by customers
8. **"33"** -- The mysterious/magnetic quality that makes people lean forward and want to learn more (named after the enigmatic "33" on Rolling Rock bottles)
9. **Trademark** -- Legal availability (10 = likely available, 5 = maybe, 0 = not available)

**Igor's scoring example for music streaming:**
| Name | Appearance | Distinctive | Depth | Energy | Humanity | Positioning | Sound | "33" | TM | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pandora | 10 | 10 | 10 | 9 | 9 | 10 | 9 | 10 | 10 | **87** |
| Spotify | 10 | 10 | 7 | 6 | 8 | 7 | 6 | 8 | 10 | **72** |
| Rhapsody | 6 | 7 | 6 | 7 | 7 | 7 | 7 | 6 | 10 | **63** |
| iHeartRadio | 5 | 4 | 5 | 5 | 5 | 5 | 5 | 5 | 10 | **49** |

### Framework 2: Alexandra Watkins -- SMILE and SCRATCH

From *Hello, My Name Is Awesome* (Inc. Magazine Top 10 Marketing Book), Watkins provides two acronym-based tests ([Nat Eliason summary](https://www.nateliason.com/notes/hello-name-awesome), [Aure's Notes](https://auresnotes.com/summary-hello-my-name-is-awesome-alexandra-watkins/)):

**SMILE (a good name should have these):**
- **S**uggestive -- makes you think of something
- **M**eaningful -- resonates with your audience
- **I**magery -- triggers a visual in the customer's mind
- **L**egs -- can be extended to other brand elements (campaigns, taglines, products)
- **E**motional -- evokes a feeling

**SCRATCH (a name should avoid these):**
- **S**pelling-challenged -- hard to spell
- **C**opycat -- too similar to competitors
- **R**estricting -- limits future growth
- **A**nnoying -- forced cleverness, hard to say
- **T**ame -- boring, vanilla, forgettable
- **C**urse of knowledge -- only insiders "get it"
- **H**ard to pronounce -- fails the phone test

### Framework 3: MetaBrand -- The Seven Criteria

A synthesis framework from MetaBrand Digital's comprehensive startup branding guide ([MetaBrand](https://www.metabrand.digital/guides/startup-branding-guide/brand-naming)):

1. **Memorable** -- sticks after one exposure
2. **Spellable** -- someone can type it correctly after hearing it
3. **Pronounceable** -- someone can say it correctly after reading it
4. **Distinctive** -- stands out from competitors
5. **Available** -- domain, trademark, social handles all clear
6. **Appropriate** -- fits category, audience, and positioning
7. **Has legs** -- can grow with the company

### Framework 4: High Alpha Venture Studio -- The Practitioner's Hierarchy

From naming 30+ software startups, High Alpha's brand lead prioritizes criteria in this order ([High Alpha / TechCrunch](https://www.highalpha.com/blog/what-i-learned-the-hard-way-from-naming-30-startups)):

1. **Ownability** (most important): .com available or attainable, search congestion low, trademark clearable, free from existing baggage
2. **Phonetic structure**: Easy to say, spell, and remember
3. **Strategic alignment** (least important): Conveys right tone, allows good brand story, doesn't limit ambitions

Key insight from High Alpha: "A good marketer can devise a good story about nearly every name. If we can find a name that's ownable, memorable, and easy to say and spell, I can make up a pretty good story that aligns to the company's vision."

### Framework 5: Lexicon Branding -- The Engineering Approach

Lexicon (behind Pentium, BlackBerry, Swiffer, Dasani, Impossible Foods) uses what they call an "engineering layer" combining proprietary software, cognitive science research, and linguistic analysis ([Lexicon Branding](https://www.lexiconbranding.com/brand-naming-process/)):

Key principles:
- **Avoid groupthink**: Small teams significantly outperform larger groups in naming. Large brainstorming sessions produce "predictable, overly clever" names
- **Make it easy to remember**: "All of us remember what we understand and what we can visualize. Mad Cow disease is memorable; Bovine Spongiform Encephalopathy is not"
- **Be noteworthy, not just different**: Being unique doesn't mean people will stop and take notice. The name must be remarkable
- **If forgettable, you lose**: "To influence a consumer's choice, you must influence what they remember"
- **Test for believability, not popularity**: When Pentium was tested, it was not the most popular candidate. But when asked about its ability to "make computers more powerful," 80% responded positively

## 6. Paul Graham and Y Combinator on Naming

Paul Graham (co-founder of Y Combinator) wrote a definitive 2015 essay on startup naming called "Change Your Name" ([paulgraham.com/name.html](https://paulgraham.com/name.html)).

### PG's Core Arguments

**1. The .com is non-negotiable for US startups.**
"If you have a US startup called X and you don't have x.com, you should probably change your name."

**2. A marginal domain signals a marginal company.**
"The problem with not having the .com of your name is that it signals weakness. Unless you're so big that your reputation precedes you, a marginal domain suggests you're a marginal company."

**3. Having x.com signals strength even if unrelated to the product.**
"As Stripe shows, having x.com signals strength even if it has no relation to what you do."

**4. The data is clear.**
- **100% of the top 20 YC companies by valuation** have their exact .com
- **94% of the top 50** have their exact .com
- Only **66% of current batch** companies had their .com (suggesting lessons ahead)

**5. Your attachment to your current name is an illusion.**
"There's nothing intrinsically great about your current name. Nearly all your attachment to it comes from it being attached to you."

**6. Naming is a distinct skill -- orthogonal to founding ability.**
"You can be a great startup founder but hopeless at thinking of names for your company."

**7. Almost any non-obviously-bad word works.**
"It turns out almost any word or word pair that is not an obviously bad name is a sufficiently good one, and the number of such domains is so large that you can find plenty that are cheap or even untaken." This is exactly how Stripe found their name -- they made a list of available domains and picked from them (their search also surfaced parse.com, which friends at Parse took).

### The Stripe Origin Story

The Collison brothers explicitly searched for available .com domains that sounded good. They didn't start with a concept or metaphor -- they started with **what was available** and then picked the best option. This is PG's recommended approach for founders who aren't naturally skilled at naming ([Quora - How did Stripe come up with its name](http://www.quora.com/How-did-Stripe-come-up-with-its-name)).

## 7. How Metaphor, Invented, and Descriptive Names Compare

### Metaphor Names (Apple, Amazon, Stripe, Nike, Slack)

**How they work**: Take a real word from one domain and apply it to an unrelated domain. The transferred associations do the heavy lifting.

| Advantage | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Instant imagery | "Apple" creates a visual immediately |
| Depth of meaning | Multiple layers: Apple = simplicity, naturalness, knowledge (Garden of Eden), Newton |
| Emotional resonance | Real words carry pre-existing emotional weight |
| Easy to pronounce | Already a known word |
| Trademark strength | Arbitrary usage = strong trademark protection (USPTO classifies as "arbitrary") |

| Disadvantage | Explanation |
|---|---|
| .com scarcity | Most good single English words are taken |
| Requires brand building | No one knows "Stripe" = payments until you tell them |
| SEO competition | Competing with the literal meaning in search |
| Can feel random | If metaphor isn't somewhat intuitive, feels disconnected |

**Why Apple worked (Igor's analysis)**: In the late 1970s, computer company names were IBM, NEC, DEC, ADPAC, Cincom, Sperry Rand, SAP. Apple needed to "reverse the entrenched view of computers" and be "simple, warm, human, approachable and different." The name Apple achieved maximum distinctiveness through category violation ([Igor Naming Guide](https://burnsclay.pbworks.com/f/Igor+Naming+Guide.pdf)).

### Invented/Coined Names (Google, Xerox, Kodak, Spotify, Häagen-Dazs)

**How they work**: Create a new word from scratch. Meaning is poured in through brand building.

| Advantage | Explanation |
|---|---|
| 100% ownable | No prior meaning = no confusion, easy trademark |
| .com available | Invented words are more likely to have open domains |
| Clean slate | You define all associations |
| Global | No unwanted meanings in other languages (if checked) |

| Disadvantage | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Zero inherent meaning | Requires massive marketing spend to build awareness |
| Can sound pharmaceutical | Many coined names (Accenture, Verizon) feel corporate and cold |
| Hard to remember | Without meaning hooks, memory has nothing to grab onto |
| Risk of feeling fake | Poorly coined names sound desperate or gimmicky |

**When invented names succeed**: When supported by sound symbolism. Swiffer works because the "sw" onset suggests swift, sweeping motion and the "-ffer" is playful and light. Google works because the double "oo" sounds expansive and fun, and "gl" has a quality of sparkling ([Lexicon Branding](https://www.lexiconbranding.com/brand-naming-process/)).

### Descriptive Names (PayPal, General Electric, Bank of America)

**How they work**: Tell you directly what the company does.

| Advantage | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Instant clarity | No confusion about what you do |
| Low marketing cost | Name does explanatory work for free |
| SEO natural | Built-in keywords |

| Disadvantage | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Weakest trademark protection | USPTO provides least protection to descriptive marks |
| Category blur | All descriptive names in a category sound alike (Igor's table of 30+ "Brand" and "Name" companies proves this) |
| Limits growth | "PayPal" is constrained if they want to do banking beyond payments |
| Easily copied | Competitors can use similar descriptive language |
| No depth | Nothing to discover, no "33" factor |

**Igor's devastating critique**: Descriptive company names "are drawn from a small pool of relevant keywords, causing them to blend together and fade into the background, indistinguishable from the bulk of their competitors -- the antithesis of marketing" ([Igor Naming Guide](https://burnsclay.pbworks.com/f/Igor+Naming+Guide.pdf)).

### The Sweet Spot: Suggestive/Evocative Names

Most naming experts recommend **suggestive names** -- names that hint at what you do without literally describing it. This is where the most successful modern startups land:

- **Slack** -- suggests cutting through friction, moving fast (but doesn't say "chat app")
- **Stripe** -- visual metaphor for payment processing (but doesn't say "payments")
- **Snowflake** -- unique data, clean/pure (but doesn't say "data warehouse")
- **Notion** -- suggests ideas, thinking (but doesn't say "workspace")
- **Canva** -- evokes "canvas" for design (but doesn't say "design tool")

## 8. B2B Infrastructure Name Analysis

### Why B2B Infrastructure Names Follow a Specific Pattern

The most successful B2B infrastructure companies share a naming profile: **short (1-2 syllables), real English words used metaphorically, with strong sensory or tactile imagery**.

### Detailed Analysis

**Stripe** (6 letters, 1 syllable)
- Origin: The Collison brothers searched for available .com domains; stripe.com was acquirable
- Metaphor layer: The magnetic stripe on credit cards; "stripes" of data flowing through payment rails
- Sound profile: Strong onset (str-) suggests strength and structure; the "i" vowel suggests precision and speed; hard "-pe" ending suggests finality/completion
- Why it works: Concrete visual image + single syllable + crisp consonant cluster

**Plaid** (5 letters, 1 syllable)
- Metaphor layer: A plaid pattern is interwoven threads -- precisely what Plaid does by weaving together financial data connections
- Sound profile: The "pl-" onset is pleasing (play, please, plenty); the "aid" ending subconsciously suggests helpfulness
- Why it works: Textile/weaving metaphor perfectly maps to connecting financial threads; short and distinctive in fintech

**Square** (6 letters, 1 syllable)
- Metaphor layer: The literal square shape of their card reader; also "square" as in fair dealing ("square deal")
- Sound profile: "Squ-" is a rare, attention-getting onset; "-are" is open and stable
- Why it works: Multi-layered meaning from one simple word; the product was literally square

**Twilio** (6 letters, 3 syllables)
- Origin: A play on "twill" (a type of fabric weave) and "IO" (input/output)
- Metaphor layer: Weaving communications together; the "io" suffix signals tech/developer orientation
- Sound profile: Soft "tw-" onset; the "il" liquid consonant flows; open "io" ending
- Why it works: The fabric metaphor subtly suggests interconnection; pronounceable as a word despite being coined

**Airtable** (8 letters, 3 syllables)
- Construction: Compound of "air" + "table"
- Metaphor layer: "Air" suggests lightness, flexibility, cloud-native; "table" grounds it in familiar data organization (spreadsheet tables, database tables)
- Sound profile: Open "air" beginning feels expansive; "table" provides grounding familiarity
- Why it works: The unexpected adjective + familiar noun combination creates intrigue while maintaining clarity

**Snowflake** (9 letters, 2 syllables)
- Metaphor layer: Each snowflake is unique (unique data); snow is white/clean/pure (clean data); snowflakes are crystalline structures (structured data)
- Sound profile: "Sn-" onset is distinctive; the "oh" vowel is expansive; "-flake" has lightness
- Why it works: Rich multi-layered metaphor where every association (unique, clean, structured, crystalline) maps to a data value proposition

**Databricks** (10 letters, 3 syllables)
- Construction: Compound of "data" + "bricks"
- Metaphor layer: Bricks are fundamental building blocks; data bricks = the building blocks of data infrastructure
- Sound profile: Strong plosives (d, b, k) convey solidity; "data" signals the domain explicitly
- Why it works: More descriptive than the others but the "bricks" metaphor adds concreteness and constructive imagery

### The Pattern

| Company | Letters | Syllables | Type | Metaphor Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stripe | 6 | 1 | Real word | Physical (credit card stripe) |
| Plaid | 5 | 1 | Real word | Textile (interwoven threads) |
| Square | 6 | 1 | Real word | Shape (card reader) |
| Twilio | 6 | 3 | Coined | Textile (twill weave) + tech (IO) |
| Airtable | 8 | 3 | Compound | Element (air) + furniture (table) |
| Snowflake | 9 | 2 | Compound word | Nature (snowflake properties) |
| Databricks | 10 | 3 | Compound | Construction (building blocks) |

**Observation**: The strongest names in B2B infrastructure use **physical/tactile metaphors** -- things you can touch, see, or feel (stripes, plaid fabric, squares, snowflakes, bricks). This makes abstract infrastructure concepts concrete and graspable.

## 9. The Tests: Cocktail Party and Beyond

### The Cocktail Party Test

Origin: The "cocktail party test" in naming refers to whether you can say your company name at a noisy cocktail party and have people understand it, remember it, and be intrigued enough to ask more. Igor calls this the **"Sound"** criterion -- the name must work when spoken aloud in casual conversation ([Igor International](https://www.igorinternational.com/process/name-evaluation-brand-evaluate-names.php)).

The test has two dimensions:
1. **Auditory clarity**: Can someone hear the name correctly in a noisy environment?
2. **Intrigue**: Does the name make someone lean forward and ask, "What's that?"

### The Full Battery of Name Tests

From synthesizing Igor, Watkins, MetaBrand, High Alpha, and Lexicon:

**1. The Tomorrow Test** (MetaBrand)
Tell someone the name once. Ask them tomorrow. Do they remember it?

**2. The Spelling Test** (MetaBrand, High Alpha)
Say the name aloud. Can someone type it correctly into a browser on the first try?

**3. The Pronunciation Test** (MetaBrand)
Show the name written. Does someone pronounce it correctly without help?

**4. The Phone Test** (Watkins -- SCRATCH)
Can you say the name over the phone and have the other person spell it correctly?

**5. The Competitive Lineup Test** (MetaBrand, Igor)
List the name alongside five competitors. Does it stand out or blend in?

**6. The Radio Test** (High Alpha)
If the name is said in a radio ad, can the listener find the website?

**7. The Three-Times-Fast Test** (Frozen Lemons)
Can someone say the name three times quickly? Difficulty signals excessive cognitive load.

**8. The Global Test** (Lexicon)
Does the name have negative connotations in any major language? In the digital world, a brand name is instantly global.

**9. The T-Shirt Test** (informal, widely used)
Would you put this name on a t-shirt and feel good about it?

**10. The "33" Test** (Igor)
Does this name have a quality that makes people lean forward and want to learn more? Is there something mysterious, surprising, or magnetically compelling about it?

## 10. Naming Trends from Crunchbase Data

Analysis from Crunchbase of hundreds of recently seed-funded startups reveals four dominant trends ([Crunchbase News](https://news.crunchbase.com/venture/startup-names-trends-cool/)):

**Trend 1: Single Dictionary Words (especially in fintech)**
The success of Stripe, Block (Square), and Plaid has cascaded through fintech. Recent examples: Context, Power, Wealth, Fierce, Decimal, Composer.

David Placek (Lexicon): "In our experience, the only trend in naming is imitation." Newer startups seek to capture the magic of existing transformative brands.

**Trend 2: Biotechs Moving Away from Pharma-Sounding Names**
Traditional: Cadrenal Therapeutics, Mineralys Therapeutics. New wave: Replay, Surf Bio, Nest Genomics. Influenced by Moderna, a name that "could work in myriad sectors beyond biotech."

**Trend 3: Creative Misspellings Persist**
Examples: Gynger, Wispr, Rezonate, Transferz, Qobra. Advantage: visually distinctive. Disadvantage: people who only hear the name won't know the unusual spelling.

**Trend 4: [Word] + AI**
Examples: Protect AI, Spice AI, Rewind AI, Lama AI. Naming experts are mixed. Placek calls it "very short-sighted" because the AI prefix will eventually become table stakes and meaningless as a differentiator.

**The overarching shift**: From invented/coined names toward **real, recognizable English words**. Simple names composed of common nouns and verbs have become the dominant pattern among recently funded startups.

## 11. Summary: The Naming Decision Matrix

### What All the Experts Agree On

These findings appear across nearly every source consulted:

1. **Shorter is better**: 4-8 characters, 1-2 syllables. Every extra syllable is a tax on memory.
2. **The .com matters enormously** (for US startups): Paul Graham's data is unequivocal.
3. **Sound matters as much as meaning**: The phonetic profile of a name creates subconscious impressions before any rational processing occurs.
4. **Suggestive/evocative beats descriptive**: Names that hint without describing outperform those that explain.
5. **Break category conventions**: The Von Restorff effect means names that violate expectations (Apple in tech, Stripe in payments) are remembered more deeply ([SpellBrand](https://spellbrand.com/blog/psychology-behind-brand-names)).
6. **Processing fluency drives preference**: If the name is easy to say, people literally like the company more and trust it more ([SpellBrand](https://spellbrand.com/blog/psychology-behind-brand-names)).
7. **Naming by committee fails**: Lexicon and High Alpha both emphasize small teams. Group brainstorming produces safe, mediocre names.
8. **A great product makes the name, not vice versa**: "If you strip away all the other brand elements, an iconic name like Google, Yahoo, or TikTok starts to look like a pretty poor company name" ([High Alpha](https://www.highalpha.com/blog/what-i-learned-the-hard-way-from-naming-30-startups)).

### The Optimal Startup Name Profile

Based on the convergence of all research:

| Dimension | Optimal | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Length | 4-8 characters | Processed as single cognitive chunk |
| Syllables | 1-2 | Maximum memorability (research: M=4.12 vs M=3.45 for longer) |
| Type | Suggestive real word or modified word | Balances meaning + ownability + trademark strength |
| Sound | Crisp consonants + energetic vowels for tech | Sound symbolism research across multiple studies |
| Domain | Exact .com | 100% of top 20 YC companies have this |
| Metaphor | Physical/tactile imagery | Makes abstract concepts concrete (see B2B infrastructure pattern) |
| Distinctiveness | Violates category naming conventions | Von Restorff effect: incongruity = deeper memory encoding |
| Emotional valence | Triggers specific emotion, not vague positivity | Emotional specificity creates stronger memory traces |

### The Sound Profile for a Tech/Infrastructure Company

Drawing from the phonetic research specifically:

- **Start with a strong consonant**: Plosives (s, t, k, p) or consonant clusters (str-, pl-, sn-) signal precision and strength
- **Include a front or central vowel**: (i, e, a) for energy and sharpness
- **End cleanly**: Hard endings (-ck, -pe, -id, -re) feel decisive and complete
- **Avoid**: Too many soft/warm sounds (m, n, l) unless targeting comfort/care categories
- **The tech sound**: Sharp, precise, efficient -- think Stripe, Slack, Pixel, Twitch, Linear, Figma
