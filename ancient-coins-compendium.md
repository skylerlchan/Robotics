# Compendium of Ancient Coins

*Compiled: April 17, 2026*

## 1. Ancient Greek Coins

The Greek world produced the first major coin systems in the West, beginning around 600 BC. The basic unit was the **drachma** (literally "a handful"), subdivided into 6 **obols**.

### Silver Denominations (Obol-Based System)

| Coin | Origin | Description | Etymology / Story |
|------|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Tetartemorion** | Athens | Smallest Athenian silver coin, worth 1/4 obol; used for the most minor daily purchases. | From Greek *tetarte* ("quarter part"). |
| **Hemitartemorion** | Athens | Worth 1/8 obol; among the tiniest coins ever struck in the ancient world, barely 5mm across. | "Half of a quarter part." |
| **Tritartemorion** | Athens | Worth 3/4 obol (three quarter-parts); a silver fraction equal to 1/8 drachma. | From *tria* ("three") + *tetartemorion*. |
| **Hemiobol** | Greek city-states | Worth 1/2 obol, or 1/12 drachma; a common small-change coin for everyday market transactions. | From *hemi* ("half") + *obolos*. |
| **Obol** | Greek city-states | The fundamental small unit, 1/6 drachma; placed in the mouths of the dead as payment to Charon the ferryman to cross the river Styx. | From *obelos* ("spit" or "nail") -- originally iron spits were used as currency; a handful of six spits became a drachma. |
| **Trihemiobol** | Greek city-states | Worth 1.5 obols; an intermediate denomination for small trade. | "Three half-obols." |
| **Diobol** | Greek city-states | Worth 2 obols, or 1/3 drachma; a common small silver coin across the Greek world. | From *di* ("two") + *obolos*. |
| **Triobol / Hemidrachm** | Greek city-states | Worth 3 obols, or 1/2 drachma; this was the standard daily wage for an Athenian juror under Pericles. | *Triobol* = "three obols"; *hemidrachm* = "half-handful." |
| **Tetrobol** | Greek city-states | Worth 4 obols, or 2/3 drachma; used in Athens and other poleis for mid-range payments. | From *tetra* ("four") + *obolos*. |
| **Pentobol** | Greek city-states | Worth 5 obols; a relatively uncommon denomination. | From *pente* ("five") + *obolos*. |
| **Octobol** | Greek city-states | Worth 8 obols; a rare larger silver fraction. | From *octo* ("eight") + *obolos*. |

### Silver Denominations (Drachma-Based System)

| Coin | Origin | Description | Etymology / Story |
|------|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Drachma** | Greek city-states (esp. Athens) | The fundamental Greek silver coin (~4.3g in the Attic standard); a skilled laborer's daily wage and the backbone of Athenian commerce and empire. | From *dragma* ("handful") -- originally a fistful of six iron obol-spits. |
| **Didrachm** | Greek city-states (esp. Corinth) | Worth 2 drachmai; the standard silver coin of Corinth, featuring Pegasus and Athena. | From *di* ("two") + *drachma*. |
| **Tridrachm** | Various Greek mints | Worth 3 drachmai; an uncommon denomination issued by some city-states. | From *tri* ("three") + *drachma*. |
| **Tetradrachm** | Athens, Macedon, Ptolemaic Egypt | Worth 4 drachmai; the workhorse trade coin of the ancient world -- the Athenian "owl" tetradrachm became the de facto international currency of the Mediterranean. | From *tetra* ("four") + *drachma*. The Athenian owl design was so trusted that other cities counterfeited it for centuries. |
| **Pentadrachm** | Ptolemaic Egypt | Worth 5 drachmai; a gold coin struck by the Ptolemies. Mentioned in ancient texts but rare in silver. | From *pente* ("five") + *drachma*. |
| **Octadrachm / Mnaieion** | Ptolemaic Egypt | Worth 8 drachmai (silver) or 100 silver drachmai (gold); the gold version was called a *mnaieion* because it equaled one mina in silver. | From *octo* ("eight"); *mnaieion* from *mna* ("mina"). |
| **Dekadrachm / Decadrachm** | Athens, Syracuse | Worth 10 drachmai; a massive, rare commemorative coin -- Syracuse's dekadrachms, signed by engravers Kimon and Euainetos, are considered the most beautiful coins ever made. | From *deka* ("ten") + *drachma*. Athens issued them after the victory at Marathon; Syracuse after defeating the Carthaginian invasion of 480 BC. |
| **Dodekadrachm** | Carthage | Worth 12 drachmai; the largest denomination in the Greek system, minted in Carthage beginning 237 BC. | From *dodeka* ("twelve") + *drachma*. |

### Gold and Electrum Denominations

| Coin | Origin | Description | Etymology / Story |
|------|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Stater** | Various (Lydia, Corinth, Macedon) | The standard gold coin; weight varied by city-state but often equaled 2 drachmai -- Philip II of Macedon's gold staters funded his conquest of Greece. | From Greek *stater* ("standard" or "weight"), implying the coin against which others were measured. |
| **Hemistater** | Various Greek mints | Half a stater; a practical gold denomination for large but not enormous transactions. | "Half-standard." |
| **Hekte** | Ionia, Lydia | Worth 1/6 stater; an electrum (gold-silver alloy) coin from the earliest period of coinage in western Anatolia. | From *hektos* ("sixth"). |
| **Hemihekte** | Ionia | Worth 1/12 stater; a tiny electrum fraction from the cities where coinage was born. | "Half of a sixth." |
| **Tetarte** | Ptolemaic Egypt | Gold quarter-stater; found in Ptolemaic documents. | From *tetartos* ("quarter"). |

### Other Greek Denominations and Units of Account

| Coin | Origin | Description | Etymology / Story |
|------|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Chalkos / Chalkous** | Greek city-states | A tiny copper coin worth 1/8 obol; the smallest copper denomination in most Greek systems. | From *chalkos* ("copper/bronze") -- the word for the metal itself became the name of the coin. |
| **Lepton** | Greek / Jewish | A tiny bronze coin meaning "small" or "thin"; later identified with the Biblical "widow's mite." | From *leptos* ("thin, small"). Became the generic term for any very small coin. |
| **Litra** | Sicily | A Sicilian unit of weight and silver coin close in size to an obol; the ancestor of the modern word "liter." | From Greek form of the Latin *libra* ("pound"). |
| **Nomos** | Magna Graecia (Greek Italy) | A name used for the silver staters of Tarentum and other southern Italian Greek cities. | From *nomos* ("custom, law, usage") -- originally meant "currency" in general. |
| **Cistophoros** | Pergamon | A large silver coin (equivalent to a tetradrachm) depicting the sacred chest (*cista mystica*) of the Dionysian Mysteries, entwined with serpents; worth 3 Roman denarii. | "Chest-bearer," from the image of the sacred basket of Dionysus on its face. |
| **Siglos** | Persia / Greek Asia Minor | A silver coin (~5.4g) of the Persian empire; the Greek form of *shekel*. | Greek adaptation of the Semitic *shekel* ("to weigh"). |
| **Mina** | Greek (adopted from Babylon) | A unit of account equal to 100 drachmai (not a coin); used in large-scale financial reckoning and temple dedications. | From Chaldean *manah* ("to count"). |
| **Talent** | Greek (adopted from Babylon) | The largest unit of account, equal to 60 minae or 6,000 drachmai (~26 kg of silver); not a coin but the measure of fortunes and state treasuries. | From Greek *talanton* ("balance, weight"). A single talent represented roughly 16 years of wages for a common laborer. |

## 2. Ancient Roman Coins

Rome built the most complex multi-metal coinage system in antiquity, evolving from crude bronze lumps to a sophisticated gold-silver-bronze hierarchy.

### Republican Period (pre-27 BC)

| Coin | Origin | Description | Etymology / Story |
|------|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Aes Rude** | Early Rome (5th-4th c. BC) | Irregular lumps of bronze used as proto-money before true coinage; valued by weight alone. | Latin: "rough bronze." |
| **Aes Signatum** | Rome (289-241 BC) | Large cast bronze ingots stamped with designs; the first "official" Roman monetary objects. | Latin: "stamped bronze." |
| **Aes Grave** | Rome (280-211 BC) | Large, heavy cast bronze coins; the first true Roman coins, featuring Janus on the *as*. | Latin: "heavy bronze." |
| **As** | Rome | The fundamental bronze unit (originally ~327g, later much lighter); depicted the two-faced god Janus -- symbolizing beginnings, transitions, and the Roman state itself. | From Latin *aes* ("bronze/copper"). Originally 12 *unciae* (ounces). |
| **Semis** | Rome | Half an *as*, featuring the head of Saturn; used for everyday small purchases. | Latin: "half." |
| **Triens** | Rome | One-third of an *as* (4 *unciae*), featuring Minerva; marked with four pellets. | Latin: "a third." |
| **Quadrans** | Rome | One-quarter of an *as* (3 *unciae*), featuring Hercules; the coin used at public baths (admission was one *quadrans*). | Latin: "a quarter." Mentioned in the Bible (Mark 12:42) as the Roman coin equaling two Jewish *lepta*. |
| **Sextans** | Rome | One-sixth of an *as* (2 *unciae*), featuring Mercury. | Latin: "a sixth." |
| **Uncia** | Rome | One-twelfth of an *as*; the smallest Republican bronze fraction and the origin of the English word "ounce." | Latin: "a twelfth part" -- the root of "ounce" and "inch." |
| **Denarius** | Rome (from 211 BC) | The principal Roman silver coin for over 400 years (~3.9g); originally worth 10 *asses*, later re-tariffed to 16 -- the coin of "Render unto Caesar" (Matthew 22:19-21). | From *denarius* ("containing ten"), since it was originally 10 *asses*. The modern words "dinar," "denier," and "penny" (abbreviated *d*) all descend from it. |
| **Quinarius** | Rome | Half a denarius (silver) or half an aureus (gold); struck intermittently as a convenience denomination. | Latin: "containing five" (originally 5 *asses*). |
| **Sestertius** | Rome | Originally a small silver coin worth 2.5 *asses*; under Augustus became a large brass coin worth 4 *asses* and the standard Roman unit of account -- prices, salaries, and fortunes were reckoned in sestertii. | From *semis-tertius* ("half-third," i.e., two and a half). |
| **Victoriatus** | Rome (c. 211-170 BC) | A silver coin worth 3/4 denarius, featuring a Victory (*Victoria*) wreath scene; used for trade with Greek southern Italy. | Named for the image of Victory (*Victoria*) on its reverse. |

### Imperial Period (27 BC onward)

| Coin | Origin | Description | Etymology / Story |
|------|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Aureus** | Rome (from ~200 BC) | The principal gold coin (~8g under Augustus), worth 25 denarii; the ultimate symbol of Roman imperial power, with the emperor's portrait guaranteeing its value across the known world. | Latin: "golden." |
| **Dupondius** | Rome | A brass coin worth 2 *asses*; distinguished from the copper *as* by its yellow orichalcum metal and the emperor's radiate crown (rays on the head). | From *duo* ("two") + *pondus* ("weight"). |
| **Sestertius** (Imperial) | Rome | Under the Empire, a large brass showpiece coin (33mm) worth 4 *asses*; featured the most elaborate reverse designs of any Roman coin and became the denomination of choice for propaganda. | Same etymology as above. The large flan gave engravers space for detailed architectural and triumphal scenes. |
| **Antoninianus** | Rome (from ~215 AD) | Introduced by Caracalla (Antoninus), a silver coin worth 2 denarii; identifiable by the emperor's radiate crown -- progressively debased until it contained almost no silver, contributing to the 3rd-century economic crisis. | Named by modern scholars after Caracalla's family name *Antoninus*. Its ancient name is unknown. |
| **Follis** | Rome (from 294 AD) | Diocletian's reformed bronze coin (~12g initially), silvered on the outside; replaced the debased antoninianus and became the workhorse base-metal coin of the late Empire. | Possibly from Latin *follis* ("bag" or "bellows"), perhaps referring to a sealed bag of coins. |
| **Argenteus** | Rome (from 294 AD) | Diocletian's pure silver coin, deliberately matching the weight and fineness of the old denarius in its prime; a nostalgia coin invoking Rome's glory days. | Latin: "silvery." |
| **Centenionalis** | Rome (4th c. AD) | A mid-sized bronze coin of the post-Diocletian era; the term appears in Roman law codes regulating coin sizes. | Possibly from *centum* ("hundred"), referring to its value in smaller units. |
| **Solidus** | Rome (from 310 AD) | Constantine's gold masterpiece (~4.5g, 72 to the Roman pound); replaced the aureus and became the most stable and trusted coin in the world for 700 years, anchoring Byzantine and medieval economies. | Latin: "solid, whole" -- implying reliability and full gold content. The word "soldier" derives from it (one paid in *solidi*). |
| **Semissis** | Rome (from ~317 AD) | Half a solidus; a gold coin for transactions where a full solidus was too much. | Latin: "half." |
| **Tremissis** | Rome (from 388 AD) | One-third of a solidus; became the most common gold coin of the late Empire and early medieval barbarian kingdoms, which continued minting imitations for centuries. | From *tremis* ("a third"). |
| **Siliqua** | Rome (from 325 AD) | A small, thin silver coin (~3.3g) introduced by Constantine; named after the carob seed used as a weight standard. | From *siliqua* ("carob pod/seed"), a natural unit of weight. The word "carat" also traces to the carob seed. |
| **Miliarensis** | Rome (from 325 AD) | A larger silver coin (~4.5g), often given as imperial donatives; its name suggests it was worth 1/1000 of a gold pound. | From *mille* ("thousand") -- one-thousandth of a pound of gold. |
| **Nummus** | Late Rome / Byzantium | The smallest bronze coin (~8-10mm), so tiny that handfuls were needed for basic purchases -- Anastasius I reformed it in 498 AD precisely because of its impracticality. | Latin/Greek: simply "coin." |
| **Maiorina** | Rome (4th c. AD) | A larger bronze coin introduced by Constans and Constantius II around 348 AD; regulated by imperial edicts. | Possibly from Latin *maior* ("larger"). |

## 3. Book of Mormon Coins (Alma Chapter 11)

The Nephite monetary system described in Alma 11 (c. 82 BC in Book of Mormon chronology) is a binary-based system of gold and silver measures tied to the value of a measure of grain. The text states these were established under King Mosiah's law and were distinct from the Jewish system brought from Jerusalem ([Alma 11:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/alma/11)). Scholars have noted the system's resemblance to ancient Egyptian and Mesopotamian weight-based systems ([ScriptureCentral](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/books/book-chapter/alma-11)).

### Gold Measures

| Measure | Value | Description |
|---------|-------|-------------|
| **Senine** | 1 (base unit) | The base gold measure; a judge's daily wage was one senine of gold (Alma 11:3), anchoring the entire system to labor value. |
| **Seon** | 2 senines | Twice the value of a senine (Alma 11:8). |
| **Shum** | 4 senines (2 seons) | Twice the value of a seon (Alma 11:9). |
| **Limnah** | 7 senines (= senine + seon + shum) | "The value of them all" -- equal to the sum of a senine, a seon, and a shum (Alma 11:10); a single coin representing the combined value of all three lesser gold denominations. |
| **Antion** | 1.5 senines (= 3 shiblons) | A gold measure "equal to three shiblons" (Alma 11:19); scholars note this name may be the root of the Zoramite city name "Antionum" and the lawyer "Antionah," possibly meaning "gold man" ([ScriptureCentral](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/books/book-chapter/alma-11)). |

### Silver Measures

| Measure | Value (in senums) | Description |
|---------|-------------------|-------------|
| **Senum** | 1 (base unit) | The base silver measure, equal in value to a senine of gold and to "a measure of barley, and also for a measure of every kind of grain" (Alma 11:7); this grain equivalence grounded the system in real commodity value. |
| **Amnor** | 2 senums | "As great as two senums" (Alma 11:11); the name *Amnor* likely shares a root with other Nephite names. |
| **Ezrom** | 4 senums (2 amnors) | "As great as four senums" (Alma 11:12); the root of the name "Zeezrom," the famous lawyer who offered Amulek 6 onties (= 42 senums) to deny God, suggesting his name roughly means "silver man" ([ScriptureCentral](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/books/book-chapter/alma-11)). |
| **Onti** | 7 senums (= senum + amnor + ezrom) | "As great as them all" (Alma 11:13); mirrors the limnah in the gold system. |

### Smaller Silver Fractions

| Measure | Value (relative to senum) | Description |
|---------|--------------------------|-------------|
| **Shiblon** | 1/2 senum | "Half of a senum; therefore, a shiblon for half a measure of barley" (Alma 11:15). |
| **Shiblum** | 1/4 senum (half a shiblon) | "A half of a shiblon" (Alma 11:16). |
| **Leah** | 1/8 senum (half a shiblum) | "The half of a shiblum" (Alma 11:17); the smallest named unit in the system. |

### Summary Table: Nephite Monetary System

| Gold | Value | Silver | Value | Grain Equivalent |
|------|-------|--------|-------|-----------------|
| Senine | 1 | Senum | 1 | 1 measure of barley |
| Seon | 2 | Amnor | 2 | 2 measures |
| Shum | 4 | Ezrom | 4 | 4 measures |
| Limnah | 7 | Onti | 7 | 7 measures |
| Antion | 1.5 | Shiblon | 0.5 | 1/2 measure |
| -- | -- | Shiblum | 0.25 | 1/4 measure |
| -- | -- | Leah | 0.125 | 1/8 measure |

The system is notably binary in its doubling pattern (1-2-4) with the top denomination being the sum of the others (1+2+4=7), and the fractions halving downward (1/2, 1/4, 1/8). The antion breaks this pattern as the only non-binary denomination.

## 4. Biblical Coins and Monetary Weights

Biblical "coins" are a mix of actual struck coins (New Testament era) and older weight-based measures (Old Testament). Many are referenced in pivotal scriptural moments.

### Old Testament Weights (Pre-Coinage)

| Name | Origin | Description | Scripture / Story |
|------|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Gerah** | Ancient Israel | The smallest Hebrew weight unit, 1/20 of a shekel; a silver measure used for sanctuary offerings. | Exodus 30:13 -- "The shekel shall be twenty gerahs." Also called *ma'ah* in Aramaic. |
| **Beka / Bekah** | Ancient Israel | Half a shekel (10 gerahs); the exact amount every Israelite male paid as a census tax ("atonement money") to the Tabernacle. | Exodus 38:26 -- "A bekah for every man, that is, half a shekel." |
| **Pim** | Ancient Israel | Two-thirds of a shekel; a weight known only from 1 Samuel 13:21, its meaning was a complete mystery until archaeologists excavated inscribed *pim* weights at Gezer in 1902-1909. | 1 Samuel 13:21 -- the Philistines charged Israelites a *pim* to sharpen their tools, a form of economic control. |
| **Shekel** | Ancient Israel / Near East | The base unit of Hebrew weight and money (~11.4g silver); the most frequently mentioned monetary unit in the Bible and still the name of Israel's currency today. | From Hebrew *shaqal* ("to weigh"). Abraham paid 400 shekels for the Cave of Machpelah (Genesis 23:15). Judas received 30 shekels for betraying Jesus (Matthew 26:15). |
| **Maneh / Mina** | Ancient Israel (from Babylon) | 50 shekels (Jewish system) or 60 shekels (Babylonian system); a weight of any metal, not a coin. | Ezekiel 45:12 -- "The mina shall be fifty shekels." In Jesus' Parable of the Minas (Luke 19:11-27), servants are entrusted with minas to invest. |
| **Talent** (Hebrew) | Ancient Israel | 3,000 shekels or 60 minas (~34 kg); the largest biblical unit -- a staggering sum representing years of wealth. | In the Parable of the Talents (Matthew 25:14-30), the master entrusts servants with talents. The English word "talent" (meaning ability) derives from this parable. |
| **Heavy Talent** | Ancient Israel | Equal to 2 standard talents (6,000 shekels); mentioned in some biblical contexts for royal treasuries. | Used in descriptions of Solomon's wealth (1 Kings 9:14). |

### Coins of the New Testament Era

| Name | Origin | Description | Scripture / Story |
|------|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Lepton / Mite** | Jewish (Hasmonean) | The tiniest bronze coin in circulation (~1g); the famous "widow's mite" -- Jesus praised a poor widow who gave two *lepta* (all she had) over the large gifts of the rich. | Mark 12:41-44 -- "She out of her poverty has put in everything she had." Two lepta equaled one Roman *quadrans*. |
| **Prutah** | Jewish (Hasmonean) | A small bronze coin roughly equal to a Roman *quadrans*; the common coin of the poor in Judea, approximately equal to 2 lepta. | Minted under Hasmonean and Herodian rulers. Crudely struck, often off-center. |
| **Shekel of Tyre** | Phoenicia / Judea | A silver tetradrachm (~14g) minted in Tyre; despite bearing the pagan god Melqart, it was the *required* coin for the Temple tax because of its high silver purity (>95%). | Matthew 17:27 -- the "stater" found in the fish's mouth was likely a Tyrian shekel, worth exactly two Temple tax payments (one for Jesus, one for Peter). |
| **Denarius** | Rome (used in Judea) | The standard Roman silver coin; the day's wage for a laborer and the coin Jesus used to teach about earthly vs. divine authority. | Matthew 22:19-21 -- "Render unto Caesar the things that are Caesar's." Also the wage in the Parable of the Workers in the Vineyard (Matthew 20:2). |
| **Drachma** | Greek (used in Judea) | A Greek silver coin roughly equivalent to a denarius; the coin a woman desperately searches for in Jesus' parable. | Luke 15:8-10 -- the Parable of the Lost Coin: a woman loses one of her ten drachmas and sweeps the entire house to find it, then calls her neighbors to rejoice. |
| **Didrachma** | Greek (used in Judea) | A two-drachma coin; the exact amount of the annual Temple tax owed by every Jewish male over 20. | Matthew 17:24 -- "Does your teacher not pay the two-drachma tax?" |
| **Stater** | Greek / Tyrian | A four-drachma coin (tetradrachm); in the most whimsical miracle in the Gospels, Jesus tells Peter to catch a fish and find a stater in its mouth to pay the Temple tax for both of them. | Matthew 17:27 -- "Go to the sea, cast a hook... you will find a stater. Take that and give it to them for me and for yourself." |
| **Assarion** | Rome / Greek East | The Greek name for the Roman *as*; two sparrows were sold for a single assarion in Jesus' teaching about God's care for even the smallest creatures. | Matthew 10:29 -- "Are not two sparrows sold for an assarion?" Luke 12:6 values five sparrows at two assaria. |
| **Quadrans** | Rome | The smallest Roman bronze coin (1/4 *as*); Jesus referenced it in teaching about settling debts. | Matthew 5:26 -- "You will not get out until you have paid the last quadrans." |
| **Kodrantis** | Greek form of *quadrans* | The Greek transliteration of the Roman *quadrans*; used in Mark's Gospel to explain the widow's two lepta to a Roman audience. | Mark 12:42 -- "Two lepta, which make a kodrantis." |
| **Thirty Pieces of Silver** | Judea | The price of Judas's betrayal, likely 30 Tyrian shekels (~480g silver); also the Old Testament price of a slave (Exodus 21:32) and the amount Zechariah was paid and threw into the Temple treasury. | Matthew 26:15 -- Judas asks "What will you give me if I deliver him over to you?" They paid him thirty pieces of silver. Zechariah 11:12-13 prophesied this amount. |

## 5. Other Ancient Civilizations

### Lydian Coins (First Coins Ever Minted)

| Coin | Origin | Description | Etymology / Story |
|------|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Lydian Stater** | Lydia (modern Turkey), c. 610-600 BC | The first true coins in human history, made from electrum (natural gold-silver alloy) and later pure gold under King Croesus; featured a lion and bull facing each other, symbolizing royal power. | Croesus's gold staters were so famous that "rich as Croesus" became a proverb. His innovation of separating gold and silver into distinct coinages created the world's first bimetallic monetary system. ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/money/coin)) |
| **Lydian Trite / Hekte** | Lydia | A 1/3 or 1/6 stater of electrum; among the earliest coins, featuring a lion's head -- possibly the first coins ordinary people could use. | The Lydian trite is often cited as the oldest known coin type, dating to c. 610 BC. |

### Persian Coins

| Coin | Origin | Description | Etymology / Story |
|------|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Daric** | Achaemenid Persia (from c. 520 BC) | A high-purity gold coin (~8.4g) showing the Great King as an archer; one of the first international gold currencies, it helped Persia fund armies and bribe Greek politicians. | Likely from Old Persian *dariya-ka* ("golden"), though popularly associated with King Darius I. The Spartans joked that they were defeated not by Persian swords but by "Persian archers" -- meaning darics. ([CoinWeek](https://coinweek.com/the-daric-persian-gold-coinweek-ancient-coin-series/)) |
| **Siglos** | Achaemenid Persia | The silver counterpart to the daric (~5.4g), also depicting the Great King as an archer; 20 sigloi equaled one daric, forming the first true bimetallic standard. | Greek form of *shekel*. Together, the daric and siglos formed what scholars call the first bimetallic monetary standard in history. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_coinage)) |
| **Double Daric** | Achaemenid Persia | A rare, heavy gold piece worth 2 darics; struck for special occasions or treasury hoards. | Same root as daric. |

### Egyptian

| Measure | Origin | Description | Etymology / Story |
|---------|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Deben** | Ancient Egypt | Not a coin but a unit of weight (~91g) used as money of account; goods were priced in debens of copper, silver, or gold, and trade was conducted by weighing metal against the deben standard. | Egypt did not mint coins until the Ptolemaic period (after Alexander's conquest). The deben system persisted for millennia as a weight-based barter economy. |
| **Kite** | Ancient Egypt | A smaller weight unit equal to 1/10 of a deben (~9.1g); used for finer transactions. | The kite and deben formed Egypt's two-tier weight-money system for over 2,000 years before coinage arrived. |

### Chinese

| Coin | Origin | Description | Etymology / Story |
|------|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Ban Liang** | Qin Dynasty, China (from 336 BC) | A round bronze coin with a distinctive square center hole; the standard currency of China's first unified empire under Qin Shi Huang -- its shape (round heaven, square earth) became the template for Chinese coins for 2,000 years. | *Ban liang* means "half a liang" (a unit of weight equal to ~7.8g). The square hole allowed coins to be strung on cords for easy transport. |
| **Wu Zhu** | Han Dynasty, China (from 118 BC) | A bronze coin weighing ~3.5g with a square hole; replaced the ban liang and became the longest-lived coin type in Chinese history, minted for over 700 years. | *Wu zhu* means "five zhu" (a *zhu* being 1/24 of a *liang*). |
| **Knife Money** | Qi and Yan states (pre-Qin China) | Bronze coins shaped like miniature knives, sometimes 15cm long; regional currency of northeastern Chinese states before unification. | Thought to derive from actual knife-tools used in barter. |
| **Spade Money** | Zhou Dynasty states (pre-Qin China) | Bronze coins shaped like miniature agricultural spades; used in central Chinese states. | Derived from the shape of spade farming tools, reflecting China's agricultural economy. |

### Indian

| Coin | Origin | Description | Etymology / Story |
|------|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Karshapana / Pana** | Mahajanapada kingdoms, India (c. 600 BC) | Among the earliest Indian coins; small silver pieces punch-marked with symbols (sun, moon, animals, trees) by merchants and kings -- no two are identical. | From Sanskrit *karsha* (a weight unit) + *pana* ("money"). The punch-marked karshapana system predates the Maurya Empire. |
| **Nishka** | Vedic India | An ancient gold ornament-weight mentioned in the Rig Veda; possibly the oldest reference to gold-as-money in Indian civilization. | Sanskrit: possibly "gold piece." Referenced in the oldest Indian texts as a unit of wealth. |

### Celtic

| Coin | Origin | Description | Etymology / Story |
|------|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Celtic Stater** | Gaul, Britain, Central Europe (3rd-1st c. BC) | Gold and silver coins imitating Philip II of Macedon's staters but with increasingly abstract, swirling Celtic art; among the most visually distinctive ancient coins. | Celtic warriors returning from mercenary service in Greece and Macedon brought staters home, then local mints began copying them -- each generation more stylized until the original design was unrecognizable. |
| **Celtic Potin** | Gaul, Britain | Cast bronze coins made from tin-rich alloy; mass-produced by pouring metal into clay molds rather than striking with dies. | From French *potin* ("pot-metal"), referring to the casting technique. |

### Phoenician

| Coin | Origin | Description | Etymology / Story |
|------|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Phoenician Shekel** | Tyre, Sidon, Byblos | Silver coins of the Phoenician city-states; the Tyrian shekel became the most trusted silver coin in the eastern Mediterranean due to its consistent ~95% purity -- so trusted it was required for the Jewish Temple tax despite bearing the image of Melqart (Baal). | From the same Semitic root *shaqal* ("to weigh"). The irony of a pagan coin being required for Temple worship troubled later rabbis. ([Three Thirty Ministries](https://threethirtyministries.com/the-shekel-of-tyre/)) |

### Parthian and Sasanian

| Coin | Origin | Description | Etymology / Story |
|------|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Parthian Drachm** | Parthian Empire (247 BC - 224 AD) | Silver coins featuring the Parthian king in profile; continued the Greek drachm tradition but with distinctive Parthian royal imagery. | The Parthians adopted Greek coinage standards from the Seleucids they conquered. |
| **Sasanian Drachm** | Sasanian Empire (224-651 AD) | Large, thin silver coins (~4g) with the king's portrait and a Zoroastrian fire altar; the most commonly found ancient coins in the Middle East, minted in enormous quantities. | The Sasanian drachm was so ubiquitous that early Islamic rulers initially just overstruck them with Arabic inscriptions rather than designing new coins. |

## 6. Byzantine Coins

The Byzantine (Eastern Roman) Empire maintained a remarkably stable coinage for nearly a millennium, centered on the gold *solidus/nomisma*.

| Coin | Origin | Description | Etymology / Story |
|------|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Solidus / Nomisma** | Byzantine Empire (from Constantine I, 310 AD) | The gold standard of the medieval world (~4.5g, 24 carats); maintained its purity for 700 years (the longest-running stable currency in history), accepted from Britain to China. | *Solidus* = Latin "solid"; *nomisma* = Greek "coin/money." Arab merchants called it the *bezant*, and Crusaders brought that term to Europe. The word "soldier" comes from *solidus*. ([World History Encyclopedia](https://www.worldhistory.org/Byzantine_Coinage/)) |
| **Semissis** | Byzantine Empire | Half a solidus; a gold coin for intermediate-value transactions. | Latin: "half." |
| **Tremissis** | Byzantine Empire | One-third of a solidus; the most practical gold coin for daily use in the early Byzantine period. | Latin: "a third." |
| **Histamenon** | Byzantine Empire (from 960s) | A full-weight gold nomisma, introduced to distinguish it from the lighter *tetarteron*; eventually became concave (cup-shaped) in the 11th century. | From Greek *histanai* ("to stand/to weigh correctly"), emphasizing it was the "proper weight" coin. |
| **Tetarteron** | Byzantine Empire (from 960s) | A lightweight gold coin, approximately 2/3 the weight of a full nomisma; introduced by Nikephoros II Phokas, reportedly to pay soldiers less while appearing to pay the same. | From Greek *tetartos* ("fourth"), though it was actually about 2/3 weight -- the name may reflect a complex accounting relationship. |
| **Hyperpyron** | Byzantine Empire (from 1092) | The reformed gold coin of Alexios I Komnenos (~4.45g, ~20.5 carats); replaced the debased nomisma and became the new gold standard, though itself gradually debased over the following centuries. | From Greek *hyperpyron* ("super-refined," literally "above fire"), referring to the purification of gold by fire. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperpyron)) |
| **Aspron Trachy** (electrum) | Byzantine Empire (from 1092) | A cup-shaped (concave) electrum coin worth 1/3 of a hyperpyron; approximately 25% gold and 75% silver. | *Aspron* = "white" (referring to silver color); *trachy* = "rough" (referring to the uneven cup shape). |
| **Aspron Trachy** (billon) | Byzantine Empire (from 1092) | A cup-shaped billon (base silver) coin worth 1/48 of a hyperpyron; the common coin of everyday Byzantine commerce. | Same etymology; the billon version was the "small change" of the reformed system. |
| **Follis** (Byzantine) | Byzantine Empire (from 498 AD) | Anastasius I's reformed large bronze coin worth 40 *nummi*; marked with a large **M** (Greek numeral for 40) on the reverse. | Same root as the Roman follis. The clear value markings (M=40, K=20, I=10, E=5) were Anastasius's innovation to eliminate confusion. |
| **Half-Follis** | Byzantine Empire | Worth 20 *nummi*, marked with **K** (Greek for 20); the second-largest bronze denomination. | |
| **Decanummium** | Byzantine Empire | Worth 10 *nummi*, marked with **I** (Greek for 10). | From Latin *decem* ("ten") + *nummus*. |
| **Pentanummium** | Byzantine Empire | Worth 5 *nummi*, marked with **E** (Greek for 5); the smallest practical bronze coin. | From Greek *pente* ("five") + *nummus*. |
| **Hexagram** | Byzantine Empire (from 615 AD) | A silver coin (~6.8g) introduced by Heraclius to fund wars against Persia; one of the few regularly issued Byzantine silver coins. | From Greek *hex* ("six") + *gramma* ("unit of weight"), i.e., "six grammata." |
| **Miliaresion** | Byzantine Empire (from c. 720 AD) | A silver coin introduced by Leo III; initially ceremonial, it became the standard silver denomination from c. 830 until the late 11th century. | From Latin *miliarensis*, itself from *mille* ("thousand"). |
| **Stavraton** | Late Byzantine Empire (from 1367) | A large silver coin introduced by John V Palaiologos; one of the last Byzantine coin types, minted as the empire crumbled. | From Greek *stavros* ("cross"), for the cross prominently featured on it. |
| **Basilikon** | Byzantine Empire (from 1304) | A silver coin introduced by Andronikos II, depicting Christ and the emperor. | From Greek *basilikos* ("royal/imperial"). |

## 7. Medieval Coins

The medieval period saw coinage evolve from Charlemagne's simple silver penny system to the great gold coins of the Italian Renaissance.

### Charlemagne's System and Its Descendants

| Coin | Origin | Description | Etymology / Story |
|------|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Denier / Denaro / Pfennig / Penny** | Frankish Empire (from c. 755 AD) | The universal silver coin of early medieval Europe; Charlemagne standardized it at ~1.7g and it became the only coin minted in Western Europe for 500 years -- every other denomination was just a unit of account. | From Latin *denarius*. The English *penny* (abbreviated *d* for *denarius* until 1971), French *denier*, Italian *denaro*, and German *pfennig* are all descendants. |
| **Sou / Solidus / Shilling** | Frankish system (unit of account) | Worth 12 deniers; NOT a coin but a unit of account in Charlemagne's system -- it took 500 years before an actual coin worth a shilling was minted. | From Latin *solidus*. The English shilling (abbreviated *s* for *solidus*) persisted until decimalization in 1971. |
| **Livre / Pound / Lira** | Frankish system (unit of account) | Worth 20 sous or 240 deniers; a pound of silver divided into coins -- also purely a unit of account until the English sovereign appeared in 1489. | From Latin *libra* ("pound, balance"). The British pound sign (**L**) is a stylized *L* for *libra*. |

### English Medieval Coins

| Coin | Origin | Description | Etymology / Story |
|------|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Penny** | Anglo-Saxon England (from c. 600 AD) | The base English silver coin for nearly 1,000 years; early pennies featured the king's name and a cross on the reverse (the cross served as a cutting guide to make halfpennies and farthings). | People literally cut pennies in half and quarters along the cross lines to make change, which is why the halfpenny and farthing were eventually minted separately. |
| **Farthing** | England | Worth 1/4 penny; among the smallest coins in medieval England. | From Old English *feorthing* ("fourth part"). |
| **Halfpenny** | England (from c. 1100) | Worth 1/2 penny; originally made by cutting a penny in half before being minted as a proper coin. | |
| **Groat** | England (from 1279) | A larger silver coin worth 4 pence; introduced by Edward I to handle inflation and trade growth that made the penny too small for many transactions. | From Old French *gros* ("large, thick"), via the Italian *grosso*. |
| **Half-Groat / Dandyprat** | England | Worth 2 pence; later nicknamed a *dandyprat* in the 16th century. | A *dandyprat* was also slang for a small, insignificant person. |
| **Noble** | England (from 1344) | The first major English gold coin, worth 80 pence (6 shillings 8 pence); featured Edward III standing in a ship, commemorating the English naval victory at Sluys (1340). | Called "noble" because gold coins were associated with nobility. The ship design celebrated England's claim to naval supremacy. ([Medieval Britain](https://medievalbritain.com/type/guides-articles/coins-of-medieval-britain-the-complete-list-with-pictures/)) |
| **Angel** | England (from 1465) | A gold coin worth 80 pence featuring the Archangel Michael slaying a dragon; believed to have healing powers -- English monarchs distributed "touch pieces" (angels) to cure scrofula ("the King's Evil"). | Inspired by the French *angelot*. The superstition that angels could cure disease persisted into the 18th century. |
| **Rose Noble / Ryal** | England (from 1464) | A gold coin worth 120 pence (10 shillings); an upvalued successor to the noble, featuring a rose. | Edward IV increased the noble's value and renamed it. |
| **Crown** | England (from 1526) | A gold coin worth 5 shillings (1/4 pound); introduced by Henry VIII, inspired by the French *ecu*. | Named for the royal crown featured on the design. |
| **Sovereign** | England (from 1489) | The first English coin worth a full pound (20 shillings); introduced by Henry VII as a prestige denomination showing the king enthroned. | From "sovereign" (supreme ruler). |
| **Leopard** | England (1344) | A short-lived gold coin worth 72 pence (6 shillings) issued by Edward III; intended for European trade but quickly abandoned. | Featured a crowned leopard (actually a heraldic lion), symbolizing English royal power. |

### Continental European Medieval Coins

| Coin | Origin | Description | Etymology / Story |
|------|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Grosso / Groschen / Gros Tournois** | Italy / Germany / France (from 1172) | A large silver coin worth multiple pennies; Venice's *grosso* (1172) broke the penny monopoly and revolutionized European commerce by providing a coin large enough for serious trade. | From Latin *grossus* ("thick, large"). The Venetian grosso was the first European silver coin larger than a penny in 500 years. |
| **Florin** | Florence (from 1252) | The first major gold coin of medieval Europe (~3.5g); featured a lily (*fiorino*) on one side and John the Baptist on the other -- it became the international trade currency of the 13th-14th centuries. | From *fiorino d'oro* ("little golden flower"), after Florence's lily emblem. Florence's banking families (Bardi, Peruzzi, Medici) spread the florin across Europe. ([VCoins](https://www.vcoins.com/en/coins/world/medieval-1778.aspx)) |
| **Ducat** | Venice (from 1284) | Venice's answer to the florin; a gold coin (~3.5g) featuring Christ in a mandorla and the Doge kneeling before St. Mark -- it eventually overtook the florin as Europe's premier trade coin and was minted across the continent. | From Latin *ducatus* ("duchy"), originally referring to the Duchy of Apulia. The ducat maintained its weight and purity for over 500 years. Shakespeare mentions ducats more than any other coin. |
| **Ecu** | France (from 1266) | A gold coin featuring a shield (*ecu*) with the royal arms; the prestige gold coin of the French monarchy. | From French *ecu* ("shield"). |
| **Franc** | France (from 1360) | A gold coin created to pay the ransom of King John II ("the Good") after his capture by the English at the Battle of Poitiers; its name celebrated his liberation. | From the inscription *FRANCORUM REX* ("King of the Franks"), but also punning on *franc* ("free") -- because the coin purchased the king's freedom. |
| **Genovino** | Genoa (from 1252) | Genoa's gold coin, minted the same year as Florence's florin; featured a gateway (Genoa's emblem) and helped fund Genoese maritime trade. | From "Genova" (Genoa). |
| **Augustalis** | Kingdom of Sicily (from 1231) | A gold coin struck by Holy Roman Emperor Frederick II; the first gold coin minted in Western Europe since the Carolingians, featuring a Roman-style imperial portrait. | From *Augustus*, the Roman imperial title Frederick II claimed. It deliberately evoked ancient Roman aureus coins. |
| **Tari** | Islamic Sicily / Southern Italy | A gold coin of Arabic origin used across the medieval Mediterranean; adapted from the Islamic *dinar* tradition. | From Arabic *tarih*. Norman rulers of Sicily continued minting taris with Arabic inscriptions. |
| **Thaler / Taler** | Bohemia / Germany (from 1519) | A large silver coin minted from silver from the mines of Joachimsthal in Bohemia; the direct ancestor of the US "dollar." | Short for *Joachimsthaler* ("from Joachim's valley"). The word evolved: *thaler* to *daler* (Dutch) to *dollar* (English). Every dollar in your pocket traces its name to a 16th-century Bohemian mining town. |
| **Guinea** | England (from 1663) | A gold coin originally worth 20 shillings, made from gold imported from the Guinea coast of West Africa by the Royal African Company; its value fluctuated with gold prices. | Named for the Guinea region of West Africa, the source of the gold. The guinea became a prestige denomination -- professionals quoted fees in guineas (21 shillings) rather than common pounds. |
| **Bezant** | Crusader States / European term | The Western European name for the Byzantine gold solidus/nomisma; used across Crusader coinage and became a heraldic symbol (a gold roundel on a coat of arms). | From "Byzantium." Crusaders minted their own imitation bezants in the Holy Land. |
