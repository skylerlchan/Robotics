## 1. Professional Naming Agency Methodologies

### Lexicon Branding (David Placek, Founder)

Lexicon is the most storied naming agency in the world, responsible for names like **Sonos, BlackBerry, Pentium, Swiffer, PowerBook, Dasani, Azure, Vercel, Windsurf, and Impossible Burger**. Over 40 years, they have named nearly 4,000 brands, employing over 250 linguists ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek)).

**Lexicon's Three-Step Process:**

1. **Create** -- Generate a massive volume of raw name candidates. Lexicon routinely begins with 2,000-3,000 ideas and bans all evaluation during the generation phase. Quantity is critical because legal clearance, linguistic filters, and strategy criteria will eliminate 90% of candidates. The philosophy: "You need 1,000 to 1,500 names before you'll find gems." ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek))

2. **Invent** -- Evaluate and refine the strongest candidates. Lexicon uses speculative discussion -- "What could this name do for us?" -- before allowing any thumbs-down. The discipline of deferring critique protects originality from premature death.

3. **Implement** -- Test finalists against market positioning, trademark viability, and cross-cultural linguistics. Then build the narrative and rationale that will launch the name.

**The Three-Team Method:**
Lexicon's signature innovation is splitting the naming work across three independent teams with different briefs ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek)):
- **Team 1** knows the real product brief
- **Team 2** is told they are naming a competitor's product
- **Team 3** works on an entirely unrelated category (e.g., told they are naming a bicycle when the real product is a coding IDE)

This forces divergent thinking. The name **Windsurf** (Codeium's AI coding IDE) came from Team 3, which was just listing "flow" concepts without any knowledge of what an IDE was.

**The Diamond Framework (DIY version):**
Draw a diamond shape and label four points ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek)):
- **Top: "Win"** -- What does winning look like for this brand?
- **Right: "What we have to win"** -- Current assets and advantages
- **Bottom: "What we need to win"** -- Gaps to fill
- **Left: "What we need to say"** -- Core message and positioning

This shifts the focus from finding words to defining behavior and experience, which is where breakthrough names emerge.

**Key Lexicon Principles:**
- Great names make you uncomfortable. Sonos was initially rejected for "not being entertainment enough." Azure was called "dumb." BlackBerry founders thought the name was "crazy." **If your team is comfortable with a name, you probably don't have the right name.** ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek))
- You won't "know it when you see it." In 4,000 naming projects, clients essentially never have an instant aha moment.
- Look for names that are **polarizing** -- when Intel's executive committee split between "Pro Chip" and "Pentium," CEO Andy Grove said: "I see the polarization here...that tells me there's energy for Pentium." ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek))
- Use the **"competitor test"** to validate bold names: instead of asking "What do you think of this name?", say "Our competitor just launched with this name -- what's your reaction?" This reveals what the name actually does in the marketplace. ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek))

### Igor Naming Agency (Steve Manning, CEO)

Igor, based in Sausalito, California (founded 2002), is known for its "almost militant embrace" of using **real, natural-sounding words** in naming. Their clients include Gogo Inflight, Cutthroat Kitchen, TruTV, and the Aria Resort ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igor_Naming_Agency)).

**Igor's Core Philosophy:**
Igor is vocally critical of the broader naming industry. Their influential "Beware the Happy Idiot" framework identifies three traps agencies fall into ([Igor International Blog](https://igorinternational.com/blog/2024/08/27/naming-agency-naming-agencies-3/)):

1. **The Happy Idiot** -- The agency invents a meaningless word, then uses "morpheme addiction" to convince the client it has deep meaning by breaking it into roots from multiple languages. Igor's verdict: "Having a meaning doesn't make a name meaningful." If the rationale requires a linguistics PhD to explain, the name won't work.

2. **The Happy Idiot with a Passport** -- Using real words from foreign languages unfamiliar to the target audience. The meaning only exists in a language the customer doesn't speak, making it functionally meaningless.

3. **The Happy Idiot with a Wallflower** -- Combining overused branding words (Active, Arc, Atlas, Blue, Bridge, Core, Edge, Flex, Flow, Hub, Next, Path, Pulse, Smart, Wave, Zip) into forgettable compound names. Igor's list of one agency's output: Bridgescape, Bridgespan, Everbridge, Flybridge, Gainbridge, PSI Bridge -- all generic, all forgettable.

**Igor's Alternative:**
Names should work based on their **look, sound, and personality** without needing explanation. Real words used in surprising contexts create the strongest brands (Apple, Amazon, Virgin, Orange).

### A Hundred Monkeys

Known for conceptual, story-driven naming. Their process emphasizes understanding the brand's narrative before generating any names. They work in small, interdisciplinary teams and focus on names that carry an inherent story or metaphor.

### Catchword

Catchword specializes in systematic, structured naming processes with particular strength in tech and healthcare naming. They emphasize audience testing and quantitative validation alongside creative generation.

## 2. Linguistic Techniques

### Sound Symbolism

Sound symbolism is the scientifically proven phenomenon where the audible properties of a name -- how it sounds to our ears without semantic clues -- create psychological impressions. David Placek (Lexicon) calls this the foundation of world-class naming ([Branding Mag](https://www.brandingmag.com/david-placek/the-science-of-sound-symbolism-and-the-importance-of-your-brand-name/)).

**Key findings from Lexicon's proprietary research** ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek)):
- **"V"** is the most alive/vibrant sound -- Corvette, Viagra, Vercel
- **"B"** is the most reliable sound -- BlackBerry
- **"Z"** draws attention and signals precision -- Azure
- **"X"** signals innovation and cutting-edge technology -- Xerox
- **Hard consonants (k, t, p)** suggest precision, energy, action -- Kodak, TikTok
- **Soft consonants (m, l, r, n, h, w)** suggest comfort, luxury, gentleness -- Marriott, Hilton, Loreal, Revlon

**Industry-specific sound patterns** ([Branding Mag](https://www.brandingmag.com/david-placek/the-science-of-sound-symbolism-and-the-importance-of-your-brand-name/)):
- **Automotive** (emphasizing speed, action, toughness): letters j, p, sh, k, v -- Porsche, Jaguar, Corvette
- **Hotels** (emphasizing comfort, luxury): letters m, h, w, l, r, n -- Marriott, Hilton, Wyndham, Hyatt
- **Cosmetics** (emphasizing luxury, sensuality): same gentle consonants -- Loreal, Maybelline, Aramis, Revlon

**Sound symbolism is cross-cultural.** When tested against other languages, sound symbolism properties stayed consistent -- critical for brands desiring global reach ([Branding Mag](https://www.brandingmag.com/david-placek/the-science-of-sound-symbolism-and-the-importance-of-your-brand-name/)).

### The Bouba/Kiki Effect

One of the most replicated findings in cognitive science. When shown a spiky shape and a rounded shape, people across all cultures consistently assign the name "Kiki" to the spiky shape and "Bouba" to the rounded one. This demonstrates that sounds carry inherent visual/tactile meaning ([Neuroscience Of](https://www.neuroscienceof.com/branding-blog/consumer-psychology-product-naming-marketing)):

- **Bouba sounds** (rounded vowels o, u + soft consonants b, m, l): feel soft, rounded, luxurious, comforting
- **Kiki sounds** (sharp vowels i, e + hard consonants k, t, p): feel sharp, precise, energetic, angular

**Application to naming:**
- Products conveying softness and luxury adopt names with flowing sounds -- Bouba qualities
- Products projecting speed, power, or energy incorporate sharp consonants -- Kiki qualities
- "Balloon," "moon," "globe" -- soft consonants match rounded concepts
- "Crack," "tick," "spike" -- hard consonants match angular concepts

### Phonesthemes

Phonesthemes are sound clusters that carry consistent meaning across multiple words in a language:
- **gl-** relates to light/vision: glow, gleam, glitter, glint, glare
- **sn-** relates to the nose: snore, sniff, sneeze, snout
- **-ump** relates to roundness: bump, lump, dump, plump
- **sl-** relates to smooth/slippery: slide, slip, slick, slim

These can be leveraged in coined names to create subconscious meaning.

### Morpheme Combinations

Morphemes are the smallest meaningful units of language. Naming professionals combine morphemes from Latin, Greek, and modern languages to build new words with embedded meaning:
- **Pentium** = "pent" (five, for the fifth-generation processor) + "-ium" (element/substance suffix suggesting foundational importance)
- **Accenture** = "accent" + "future"
- **Verizon** = "veritas" (truth) + "horizon"

**Warning from Igor:** Morphemic rationale can become "morphemic pretzel logic" when agencies string together meanings from multiple languages foreign to the audience. An invented name has to work on its own, without explanation ([Igor International Blog](https://igorinternational.com/blog/2024/08/27/naming-agency-naming-agencies-3/)).

### Portmanteaus

Blending two words into one new word:
- **Netflix** = internet + flicks
- **Instagram** = instant + telegram
- **Microsoft** = microcomputer + software
- **Comcast** = communications + broadcast
- **Pinterest** = pin + interest
- **Groupon** = group + coupon

### Compound Words

Two complete words merged:
- **PowerBook** = power + book
- **Facebook** = face + book
- **BlackBerry** = black + berry
- **Windsurf** = wind + surf
- **LinkedIn** = linked + in
- **FedEx** = federal + express

Lexicon's research shows compounds are "cognitive multipliers" -- they create **"1 + 1 = 3" associations** in the brain. Despite being longer, they are more memorable because they create richer mental networks ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek)).

### Neologisms (Coined Words)

Completely invented words with no prior dictionary meaning:
- **Kodak** -- George Eastman wanted a strong, incisive word starting and ending with K
- **Xerox** -- from the Greek "xeros" (dry), suggesting dry printing
- **Häagen-Dazs** -- completely made up to sound Danish (the umlaut doesn't even exist in Danish)
- **Etsy** -- founder wanted a "nonsensical word" to build from scratch; heard while watching Fellini's "8 1/2"

### Visual Linguistics

How a name looks when spelled out matters as much as how it sounds ([Branding Mag](https://www.brandingmag.com/david-placek/the-science-of-sound-symbolism-and-the-importance-of-your-brand-name/)):
- **Letter doubling** influences perception: "Google" looks friendlier than "Gugle" even though they are pronounced identically. Lexicon's research found doubling consonants in some places leads to the product being judged as having more options and features.
- **Palindromes** (Sonos, Kayak) create visual symmetry and memorability
- **Consonant-vowel patterns** (Dasani: da-SA-ni) mirror how babies learn to speak, making names intuitive across languages

### Acronyms

Compressed identifiers from longer names:
- **IKEA** = Ingvar Kamprad + Elmtaryd + Agunnaryd (founder's initials + childhood farm + village)
- **IBM** = International Business Machines
- **BMW** = Bayerische Motoren Werke
- **CVS** = Consumer Value Store

## 3. Creative Brainstorming Frameworks

### Lexicon's Volume-First Approach

The most important principle from professional naming: **separate creation from judgment** ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek)).

**Phase 1: Wild, no-filter generation** across multiple metaphorical domains. No evaluation allowed. Target: 1,000-3,000 raw ideas.

**Phase 2: Speculative discussion.** Ask "What could this name do for us?" before any thumbs-down. This protects originality from premature death.

**Phase 3: Rigorous pruning.** Apply legal, linguistic, and strategic filters.

### The Naming Brief

Every professional naming project starts with a brief that defines ([Design Your Way](https://www.designyourway.net/blog/brand-naming-process/)):
- What the name should express
- What approaches are acceptable and what's off-limits
- Target audience details
- Brand personality traits
- Competitive context
- Naming criteria and constraints
- Keep it short: 1-2 pages maximum. The brief must align all stakeholders before a single name is generated.

### Mind Mapping Method

Start with the brand's core promise at the center. Branch out into ([Design Your Way](https://www.designyourway.net/blog/brand-naming-process/)):
- Synonyms and related concepts
- Metaphors and analogies
- Emotional associations
- Industry terminology
- Latin and Greek roots
- Foreign language exploration
- Historical/mythological references
- Nature and science terms
- Sound/rhythm explorations

### Small Teams Over Large Groups

Research from Lexicon confirms: **small teams outperform large groups** in naming. Groupthink kills creativity in subjective exercises like naming ([Design Your Way](https://www.designyourway.net/blog/brand-naming-process/)).

Aim for teams of 2-4 people. Multiple small teams working independently (as in Lexicon's three-team method) produce better results than one large group.

### The Competitor Test

Lexicon's validation technique: Instead of asking "What do you think of this name?", say "Our competitor just launched with this name -- what's your reaction?" This reveals what the name actually does in the marketplace rather than getting evaluative feedback about comfort levels ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek)).

### Category Exploration Technique

For each name direction, generate ideas across all name types:
1. Descriptive names
2. Suggestive/evocative names
3. Abstract/invented names
4. Compound names
5. Real-word names from unrelated categories
6. Mythological/historical references
7. Foreign language candidates
8. Metaphorical names

## 4. Name Categories and Types

### Descriptive Names
**What:** Tells people exactly what the company does.
**Examples:** PayPal, Toys R Us, DirecTV, General Electric, The Container Store
**Pros:** Immediately understood; low marketing spend to explain
**Cons:** Extremely hard to trademark; weak differentiation; poor SEO (compete with generic terms); limit growth into new categories
**Trademark strength:** Weakest -- often rejected or only given narrow protection ([BroHouse](https://brohouse.com/blog/naming-techniques-2026-branding-strategies/))

### Suggestive/Evocative Names
**What:** Hints at what the brand does without spelling it out.
**Examples:** Uber (suggests premium), Spotify (spotting music), Amazon (vast), Slack (making work more relaxed)
**Pros:** Easier to protect legally; room for brand expansion; create emotional resonance
**Cons:** Require some marketing to establish meaning
**Trademark strength:** Moderate to strong

### Abstract/Invented Names (Neologisms)
**What:** No prior meaning in any language. Complete blank slates.
**Examples:** Kodak, Xerox, Accenture, Häagen-Dazs, Etsy
**Pros:** Guaranteed trademark protection; no competing semantic associations; own 100% of search results
**Cons:** Require significant marketing investment to build meaning from scratch
**Trademark strength:** Strongest ([BroHouse](https://brohouse.com/blog/naming-techniques-2026-branding-strategies/))

### Out-of-Category (Real Word, Unexpected Context)
**What:** A common word used in a completely unrelated industry.
**Examples:** Apple (computers), Amazon (retail), Orange (telecom), Virgin (airlines/music), Blue (ridesharing)
**Pros:** Immediately familiar; create initial cognitive challenge that, once overcome, becomes extraordinarily powerful; highly memorable
**Cons:** Require significant marketing budget; can face trademark challenges from the word's existing uses
**Trademark strength:** Strong when used outside original meaning ([BroHouse](https://brohouse.com/blog/naming-techniques-2026-branding-strategies/))

Igor considers this the strongest category: real, natural-sounding words used in surprising contexts.

### Compound Names
**What:** Two words or word fragments merged into something new.
**Examples:** FedEx, LinkedIn, Facebook, Netflix, Instagram, PowerBook, BlackBerry, Windsurf
**Pros:** More meaning than invented names; distinctive enough for strong trademark protection; "1 + 1 = 3" cognitive effect
**Cons:** Can feel corporate or forced if poorly executed
**Trademark strength:** Strong ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek))

### Founder-Based Names
**What:** Named after a person, usually the founder.
**Examples:** Ford, Disney, Adidas (Adi Dassler), Hershey, John Deere, Bloomberg
**Pros:** Build trust through personal accountability; relatively easy trademark registration; human connection
**Cons:** Complications if founder's reputation changes; ceiling on scalability; harder to communicate team concept
**Trademark strength:** Moderate ([BroHouse](https://brohouse.com/blog/naming-techniques-2026-branding-strategies/))

### Acronym Names
**What:** Letters standing for a longer name.
**Examples:** IBM, BMW, HBO, KFC, IKEA, CVS
**Pros:** Compact; clean; professional
**Cons:** Zero built-in meaning; extremely risky for new companies; nothing for people to remember
**Trademark strength:** Varies -- often weak for new brands
Igor and most professional naming agencies strongly advise against acronyms for startups ([BroHouse](https://brohouse.com/blog/naming-techniques-2026-branding-strategies/)).

### Metaphorical Names
**What:** Borrow imagery from another domain to suggest qualities.
**Examples:** Nike (Greek goddess of victory), Amazon (world's largest river for world's largest store), Patagonia (remote, rugged wilderness)
**Pros:** Rich storytelling potential; emotionally resonant; memorable
**Cons:** Meaning may not be immediately obvious
**Trademark strength:** Generally strong

## 5. Psychology of Naming

### The Fluency Effect

People prefer things with names that are **easy to bring to mind and easy to pronounce**. This is one of the most robust findings in consumer psychology ([Neuroscience Of](https://www.neuroscienceof.com/branding-blog/consumer-psychology-product-naming-marketing)):
- Politicians with simpler names win more elections
- Lawyers with fluent names are promoted to partner more quickly
- Names with jarring spellings (Zakk vs. Zach) are rated as less attractive
- **Stocks with simple, easy-to-pronounce ticker symbols** outperform newly listed companies with complex tickers (2006 study by Alter & Oppenheimer examining 300+ stocks after IPO)

### What Makes Names Memorable

Key factors from research:
1. **Distinctiveness** -- The name must stand out from category conventions. If every competitor uses "Tech" in their name, don't use "Tech."
2. **Emotional resonance** -- Names that trigger emotion are remembered more readily than neutral names. 77% of consumers say brand names directly influence purchase decisions ([Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/starting-a-business/understanding-the-psychology-behind-effective-brand-names/444819)).
3. **Phonetic pleasure** -- Names that are pleasant to say get repeated more. The consonant-vowel-consonant-vowel pattern (CVCV) is universally easy to pronounce.
4. **Concreteness** -- Names that evoke a visual image (Apple, Jaguar, Blackberry) are more memorable than abstract ones.
5. **Story potential** -- Names with an inherent narrative (Amazon = vast, Nike = victory) create deeper memory traces.
6. **Brevity** -- Jeff Johnson (who named Nike) observed that iconic brands like Clorox, Kleenex, and Xerox had short names of two syllables or less ([Jon Erlichman's Newsletter](https://jonerlichman.substack.com/p/how-43-famous-companies-got-their)).

### Emotional Connection

72% of consumers say a name aligned with a brand's mission builds a stronger emotional connection ([How Brands Are Built](https://howbrandsarebuilt.com/how-to-name-your-brand-in-2025/)). Emotion precedes logic in almost every consumer decision. A name that triggers the right emotion does half the selling work before someone opens a website ([BroHouse](https://brohouse.com/blog/naming-techniques-2026-branding-strategies/)).

### The Patagonian Toothfish Effect

One of the most dramatic examples of naming psychology: fish wholesaler Lee Lantz couldn't sell Patagonian Toothfish in America. In 1977, he renamed it "Chilean Sea Bass." Demand immediately skyrocketed. People were indifferent to Patagonian Toothfish but enthusiastic for Chilean Sea Bass -- the exact same fish ([Neuroscience Of](https://www.neuroscienceof.com/branding-blog/consumer-psychology-product-naming-marketing)).

Similarly, Goosefish became "Monkfish" and was suddenly a sought-after delicacy -- "the Foie Gras of the Sea."

### Trust and Credibility

The name is the first point of competitive differentiation. A brand name can determine if an audience will pay attention and continue to pay attention. Your brand name is a strategic piece of intellectual property -- one that is a key foundation of your brand, and should never be considered optional ([Branding Mag](https://www.brandingmag.com/david-placek/the-science-of-sound-symbolism-and-the-importance-of-your-brand-name/)).

A distinctive name "delivers **cumulative advantage**" because it is spoken, typed, and searched more than any other asset, accruing mental equity every time. Simultaneously, it grants "**asymmetric advantage**" on launch day -- before a single marketing dollar is spent -- by seizing unoccupied semantic territory ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek)).

## 6. Domain and Trademark Screening

### Domain Availability

**David Placek's 2025 guidance:** Domain availability is no longer a deal-breaker. The .com has become "just an area code." With AI changing how people search, SEO matters less. Get the right name first, then solve for domain ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek)):
- Add a prefix (get/try/use + name)
- Use alternative TLDs (.ai, .io, .co, .app)
- Negotiate to buy the .com (typical range: $15K-$30K)
- Never let domain availability drive your naming decision

### Trademark Screening Process

**Step 1: Preliminary search**
- Search USPTO (United States Patent and Trademark Office) TESS database
- Search EUIPO (European Union Intellectual Property Office)
- Search WIPO Global Brand Database
- Check state-level registrations

**Step 2: Common law search**
- Search business name registrations (Secretary of State databases)
- Google the name extensively
- Search social media handles
- Search domain registrations (WHOIS)

**Step 3: Full trademark clearance (attorney-conducted)**
- Comprehensive search across all databases
- Phonetic similarity analysis
- Visual similarity analysis
- Industry/class overlap evaluation
- Risk assessment and legal opinion

### Trademark Strength Spectrum (strongest to weakest)

1. **Fanciful/Coined** (Kodak, Xerox) -- strongest protection, guaranteed registration
2. **Arbitrary** (Apple for computers, Amazon for retail) -- strong protection
3. **Suggestive** (Uber, Netflix) -- moderate-strong protection
4. **Descriptive** (American Airlines) -- weak, often requires proof of "secondary meaning"
5. **Generic** (Computer Store) -- zero protection, cannot be trademarked

### Key Trademark Mistakes for Startups ([Ludwig IP Law](https://ludwigiplaw.com/common-trademark-mistakes-startups-avoid/))

- Registering a domain or LLC does **not** create trademark protection
- Generic or descriptive names are difficult to register and enforce
- Failing to search before investing in branding
- Not filing trademark applications early enough
- Ignoring international trademark considerations

## 7. Famous Naming Case Studies

### Google
Larry Page and Sergey Brin initially called their search engine "BackRub" (1996) because it analyzed the web's "back links." Fellow Stanford student Sean Anderson suggested "googolplex" (a very large number). Page countered with "googol." After accidentally searching for the "google" spelling of the domain, they preferred it. The parent company **Alphabet** was chosen because it represents "a collection of letters" -- and the double meaning of "alpha-bet" (investment return above benchmark) ([Jon Erlichman's Newsletter](https://jonerlichman.substack.com/p/how-43-famous-companies-got-their)).

### Nike
Originally "Blue Ribbon Sports," a U.S. distributor for Onitsuka Tiger (now Asics). When Phil Knight started manufacturing his own shoes, he liked "Dimension Six." Others suggested "Peregrine" and "Bengal." Coworker **Jeff Johnson** observed that iconic brands (Clorox, Kleenex, Xerox) had **short names, two syllables or less, with a strong sound from letters like K or X**. He pitched "Nike," the Greek winged goddess of victory ([Jon Erlichman's Newsletter](https://jonerlichman.substack.com/p/how-43-famous-companies-got-their)).

### Spotify
During a brainstorming session between co-founders Daniel Ek and Martin Lorentzon, Ek misheard one of Lorentzon's suggestions. He typed "Spotify" into Google, it was available, and they registered it. It is often said to be a blending of "spot" + "identify" ([Jon Erlichman's Newsletter](https://jonerlichman.substack.com/p/how-43-famous-companies-got-their)).

### Häagen-Dazs
Bronx entrepreneur Reuben Mattus created what he thought was a "Danish-sounding" name in the 1960s because he wanted to evoke old-world traditions and craftsmanship. He added an umlaut even though that punctuation mark doesn't exist in Danish because he felt it would make his product stand out. The name is completely fabricated -- it means nothing in any language ([Jon Erlichman's Newsletter](https://jonerlichman.substack.com/p/how-43-famous-companies-got-their)).

### Kodak
George Eastman deliberately created the name starting and ending with K because he felt K was "a strong, incisive sort of letter." He wanted a name that was short, vigorous, could not be misspelled, and meant nothing -- so it would be associated only with his product. This was in 1888, making it one of the earliest examples of intentional brand naming ([Jon Erlichman's Newsletter](https://jonerlichman.substack.com/p/how-43-famous-companies-got-their)).

### BlackBerry
Research In Motion hired Lexicon Branding a year before the device's release. The name was based on the black color of the device and the shape of its keyboard, which looked like the seeds of a berry. The founders initially thought the name was "crazy" ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek), [Jon Erlichman's Newsletter](https://jonerlichman.substack.com/p/how-43-famous-companies-got-their)).

### Sonos
Named by Lexicon. Initially rejected by the company for "not being entertainment enough." Placek recalls it eventually became synonymous with premium home audio. The name uses the palindrome structure (reads the same forwards and backwards) and the Latin root "sonus" (sound) ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek)).

### Azure (Microsoft)
Lexicon named it. Microsoft initially called it "a dumb name." It went on to become their billion-dollar cloud platform. Placek notes the "z" sound draws attention and signals precision, while the word itself evokes "the color of the sky -- limitless, vast, open" ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek)).

### Apple
Steve Jobs picked the name during one of his fruitarian diets, having just returned from an apple farm. He thought it sounded "fun, spirited, and not intimidating." A 1980 video clip adds: "I picked Apple partly because I like apples and because it's ahead of Atari in the phone book" ([Jon Erlichman's Newsletter](https://jonerlichman.substack.com/p/how-43-famous-companies-got-their)).

### Amazon
Jeff Bezos wanted words starting with "A" because early web listings were alphabetized. Amazon seemed perfect for "earth's largest bookstore" (the Amazon river is in the first logo). Several other domains were registered during the process: Awake, Browse, Bookmall, Relentless (still redirects to Amazon), and "Cadabra" (based on "abracadabra") -- which was dropped when Bezos' lawyer thought he said "cadaver" ([Jon Erlichman's Newsletter](https://jonerlichman.substack.com/p/how-43-famous-companies-got-their)).

### Instagram
Mike Krieger and Kevin Systrom initially launched a check-in app called "Burbn." Photo sharing became its most popular feature, so they pivoted and blended "instant camera" + "telegram" = Instagram ([Jon Erlichman's Newsletter](https://jonerlichman.substack.com/p/how-43-famous-companies-got-their)).

### Etsy
Founder Rob Kalin wanted "a nonsensical word because I wanted to build the brand from scratch." While watching Fellini's "8 1/2," he heard what he thought was "et si" (actually "e si" -- Italian for "oh yes") ([Jon Erlichman's Newsletter](https://jonerlichman.substack.com/p/how-43-famous-companies-got-their)).

### LEGO
In 1916, carpenter Ole Kirk Christiansen made ladders and stools. After financial disaster, he turned to making toys. "LEGO" is an abbreviation of Danish words "leg godt" meaning "play well" ([Jon Erlichman's Newsletter](https://jonerlichman.substack.com/p/how-43-famous-companies-got-their)).

### Pentium (Intel)
Named by Lexicon. "Pent" from the Greek for five (fifth-generation processor) + "-ium" suffix suggesting elemental/foundational importance. The Intel executive committee split between "Pro Chip" and "Pentium" -- CEO Andy Grove read the polarization as a signal of strength ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek)).

### Vercel
Named by Lexicon. The "V" sound is the most alive/vibrant in linguistics, and "cel" evokes "velocity" and "accelerate." The name captures the speed-first ethos of the company ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek)).

### Swiffer
Named by Lexicon for Procter & Gamble. The "sw" onset suggests sweeping motion, and the "-ffer" suffix creates a light, quick feeling. A perfect example of sound symbolism matching product function.

## 8. Common Mistakes to Avoid

### 1. Naming by Committee
Large groups produce safe, generic names. Groupthink kills creativity. Use small teams with clear decision-making authority ([Design Your Way](https://www.designyourway.net/blog/brand-naming-process/)).

### 2. Falling in Love Too Early
Don't commit before running trademark searches, domain checks, and cross-cultural vetting. Many founders invest heavily in branding around a name they can't legally own.

### 3. Being Too Descriptive
Names like "TechFlow Solutions" or "Digital Marketing Pro" are nearly impossible to trademark, compete with generic search terms, and limit future expansion. The English dictionary is "almost 99% already protected at trademark offices" ([BroHouse](https://brohouse.com/blog/naming-techniques-2026-branding-strategies/)).

### 4. Ignoring Cross-Cultural Implications
Corona, Ayds candy, Covid (the Arizona electronics company) -- all suffered from unforeseeable negative associations. Testing a name across varied contexts and markets is due diligence, not optional ([BroHouse](https://brohouse.com/blog/naming-techniques-2026-branding-strategies/), [Neuroscience Of](https://www.neuroscienceof.com/branding-blog/consumer-psychology-product-naming-marketing)).

### 5. Letting Domain Availability Drive the Decision
A weak name with a .com is worse than a strong name with a .io or a prefix. Don't let domain availability override naming quality ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek)).

### 6. Using Overused "Wallflower" Words
Avoid the thousand most common branding words: Active, Arc, Atlas, Blue, Bridge, Core, Edge, Flex, Flow, Hub, Next, Path, Pulse, Smart, Wave, Zip. Pairing two of these creates forgettable white noise ([Igor International Blog](https://igorinternational.com/blog/2024/08/27/naming-agency-naming-agencies-3/)).

### 7. Starting with an Acronym
Acronyms carry zero built-in meaning. They work for established companies (IBM, BMW) but are extremely risky for startups because there is nothing for people to remember.

### 8. Confusing "Having a Meaning" with "Being Meaningful"
A name can have a meaning in Hawaiian or Sanskrit but be functionally meaningless to your English-speaking customer. The meaning must be accessible to the audience without explanation ([Igor International Blog](https://igorinternational.com/blog/2024/08/27/naming-agency-naming-agencies-3/)).

### 9. Not Generating Enough Options
Professional agencies generate 1,000-3,000 candidates. Most founders stop at 10-20. Volume is essential because legal clearance and strategic filters will eliminate 90%+ of candidates ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek)).

### 10. Seeking Consensus Instead of Energy
The best names are polarizing, not consensus picks. If everyone in the room mildly likes a name, it's probably mediocre. Look for names that split the room -- that's where energy lives ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek)).

### 11. Evaluating During Generation
Keep creation and judgment in separate phases. Criticism during brainstorming kills the fragile, weird ideas that often become the best names.

### 12. Overthinking Pronunciation
Some of the world's most valuable brands (Porsche, Hyundai, Givenchy) are routinely mispronounced. A slight pronunciation ambiguity is not fatal. Distinctiveness matters more than perfect phonetic clarity.

## 9. Modern Naming Trends (2023-2026)

### 1. AI-Era Naming
With AI changing how people search and discover brands, SEO-driven naming is declining in importance. The name's job is shifting from "be findable via keywords" to "be memorable when spoken to an AI assistant" ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek)).

### 2. The .com is Dead (Mostly)
Placek (Lexicon) calls .com "just an area code" in 2025. Alternative TLDs (.ai, .io, .co, .dev, .app) are fully accepted. Many successful startups launch without owning the .com ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek)).

### 3. Compound Names Are Dominant in Tech
The most successful recent tech brand names are compounds: Windsurf, CapCut, Datadog, Snowflake, Figma. Compounds create richer mental associations than single words.

### 4. Real-Word Names Over Fabricated Names
A shift away from the 2010s trend of invented "-ify" and "-ly" suffixed names. The pendulum has swung toward real words used in unexpected contexts (Notion, Canva, Ripple, Stripe, Bolt, Linear, Arc) ([BroHouse](https://brohouse.com/blog/naming-techniques-2026-branding-strategies/)).

### 5. One-Word Simplicity
Short, punchy, single-word names remain powerful: Cursor, Notion, Linear, Arc, Bolt. Clean and unencumbered, these names work well in conversation with AI assistants and voice interfaces.

### 6. Storytelling-Embedded Names
Names that come with an inherent story are gaining favor. Anduril (the sword reforged in Lord of the Rings, for a defense tech company) and Anthropic (relating to humanity, for an AI safety company) carry narrative weight that extends beyond the name itself.

### 7. Sound-First Design
More founders and agencies are starting with the sound profile of the name before the meaning. Lexicon's letter-level research (V = vibrant, Z = attention, X = innovation) is becoming mainstream knowledge.

### 8. Global-First Naming
With remote work and global markets, names must work across cultures from day one. Sound symbolism's cross-cultural consistency makes it an increasingly important tool. Names are tested for negative associations in major world languages before launch.

### 9. Category-Defying Names
The most ambitious startups choose names that deliberately avoid their category's naming conventions. This is the "out-of-category" technique -- choosing a name from a completely different domain to stand out (Orange for telecom, Stripe for payments, Snowflake for data).

### 10. Emotional over Functional
The trend away from functional/descriptive naming and toward emotional/evocative naming continues to accelerate. Purchase decision psychology consistently shows that emotion precedes logic in consumer decisions ([BroHouse](https://brohouse.com/blog/naming-techniques-2026-branding-strategies/)).

## Key Takeaways

1. **Naming is not cosmetics -- it is compounding leverage.** A distinctive name accrues mental equity every time it is spoken, typed, or searched. Over years, it becomes the cheapest ROI you will ever purchase.

2. **Volume before judgment.** Generate 1,000+ candidates before evaluating. Separate creation from critique.

3. **Sound carries meaning.** Every letter has scientifically measurable psychological impact. Use sound symbolism deliberately.

4. **The best names are uncomfortable.** If everyone agrees immediately, you probably have a mediocre name. Look for polarization.

5. **A name is a vessel.** What you put in it is your story. People don't buy products -- they buy stories.

6. **Real words beat fabricated words** -- when used in surprising, out-of-category contexts.

7. **Compounds are cognitive multipliers** -- PowerBook, BlackBerry, Windsurf create "1+1=3" mental associations.

8. **Domain doesn't matter as much as you think.** Get the right name first, solve for domain second.

9. **Test with the competitor frame.** Ask "What would you think if your competitor launched with this name?" not "Do you like this name?"

10. **The further you move from descriptive words, the more human your connection with people becomes.** A coined word or unexpected real word isn't a barrier -- it's an invitation to a story you control completely.
